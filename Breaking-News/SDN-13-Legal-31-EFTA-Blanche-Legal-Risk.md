# EFTA Todd Blanche Legal-risk

### Important

This is a conditional legal-risk analysis, not an allegation that any listed person actually committed these acts.

The supplied material establishes participation/roles, not criminal or ethical wrongdoing.

A lawyer's exposure depends heavily on what the person knew, intended, did, failed to do, and had authority to do.

"Failure to investigate" or ordinary negligence is not, by itself, generally equivalent to obstruction or a federal crime.

## Risk framework

Highest criminal exposure generally arises where an attorney knowingly and corruptly:

  - conceals or destroys evidence;

  - falsifies or alters records;

  - induces a witness or victim to withhold or change testimony;

  - lies to a court, grand jury, investigators, or Congress;

  - coordinates a scheme to impede an investigation or proceeding;

  - knowingly joins another person's obstruction.

### Misleading Conduct

Federal obstruction statutes can reach witness/victim tampering, misleading conduct, concealment of evidence, and obstruction of an official proceeding. 18 USC. § 1512 can carry penalties of up to 20 years for several forms of corrupt witness/document interference, while § 1519 can carry up to 20 years for knowingly concealing, altering, falsifying, or destroying records with the requisite obstructive intent.

### Jencks Act

A prosecutor's discovery obligations are particularly significant. DOJ policy identifies Brady, Giglio, Rule 16, Rule 26.2, and the Jencks Act as core discovery authorities and says prosecutors must search the prosecution team's information for discoverable material. DOJ guidance also states that the prosecutor ultimately remains responsible for the disclosure determination.

### Professional Discipline

Prosecutorial misconduct can create consequences separate from criminal liability: professional discipline, court sanctions, adverse rulings, disqualification, reversal/vacatur issues, internal DOJ discipline, and reputational consequences.

### Best Efforts

The Crime Victims' Rights Act requires DOJ personnel engaged in detection, investigation, or prosecution to make best efforts to accord statutory victim rights, including reasonable protection, notice, a reasonable opportunity to confer with the government attorney, fairness, dignity, privacy, and reasonable access to proceedings. Willful or wanton failure can also trigger DOJ administrative consequences.

# Conduct-by-conduct Risk

## "Cover-up"

### Knowing participation

- potentially severe criminal exposure.

### Direction/authorization of subordinates

- especially significant.

### Passive receipt of information

- ordinarily insufficient.

### Failure to correct after learning of unlawful concealment

- potentially significant, depending on legal duty and intent.

### Defense counsel

- representation itself is not a cover-up; independent participation is required.

## Obstruction of justice

- Potentially one of the most serious theories.

- The precise statute depends on what was obstructed:

  - § 1512

- witnesses, victims, evidence, communications, official proceedings;

  - § 1503

- obstruction involving judicial proceedings;

  - § 1505

- obstruction involving federal agency/Congress proceedings;

  - § 1519

- records/evidence in federal investigations or matters.

- Intent matters enormously.

- Negligence, poor judgment, or an unsuccessful investigative strategy should not automatically be converted into obstruction.

## Negligence concerning victim testimony

- Usually not a standalone federal obstruction offense.

- Potential consequences can include:

  - CVRA violations;

  - DOJ administrative discipline;

  - professional discipline;

  - evidentiary/case consequences;

  - supervisory review.

- Criminal liability becomes much more plausible if "negligence" is actually evidence of intentional avoidance, concealment, retaliation, witness manipulation, or obstruction.

- The CVRA specifically requires DOJ personnel involved in detection, investigation, or prosecution to make best efforts to accord victims their statutory rights.

## Failure to investigate

- Not automatically criminal.

- Prosecutorial discretion is broad.

- The relevant question is whether the failure was:

  - a legitimate exercise of discretion;

  - negligent/incompetent;

  - deliberately indifferent to a known legal duty;

  - motivated by an improper purpose;

  - or part of an intentional obstruction/cover-up.

- The last two categories create substantially greater legal risk.

## Withholding documents during discovery

- Potentially serious for government prosecutors.

- Relevant authorities include:

  - Brady;

  - Giglio;

  - Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 16;

  - Rule 26.2;

  - 18 USC. § 3500/Jencks Act;

  - DOJ Justice Manual § 9-5.001 and § 9-5.002.

- Knowing suppression of material exculpatory/impeachment evidence can create constitutional, disciplinary, and potentially case-dispositive consequences.

- Intentional concealment with an obstructive purpose can move the issue from discovery misconduct toward criminal obstruction.

- A good-faith discovery dispute is materially different from deliberate suppression.

- For defense counsel, the government's Brady/Giglio obligation generally does not transfer to the defense.

## Destroying, altering, or concealing documents

- This is potentially more serious than simply making an incorrect discovery decision.

- If the statutory elements are satisfied, 18 USC. § 1519 can carry imprisonment of up to 20 years.

- The key question is not merely whether a document disappeared, but whether the person knowingly engaged in prohibited conduct with the required obstructive intent.

## False statements

- Knowingly material false statements to federal investigators can implicate 18 USC. § 1001.

- False testimony/declarations under oath can implicate perjury statutes.

- A mistake, misunderstanding, faulty memory, or immaterial inconsistency is not automatically perjury.

## False testimony / subornation

- A lawyer who knowingly causes or assists materially false testimony can face:

  - professional discipline;

  - obstruction exposure;

  - perjury-related theories;

  - court sanctions;

  - disqualification;

  - possible criminal liability.

- The distinction between aggressive witness preparation and knowingly procuring false testimony is critical.

## Witness/victim intimidation or manipulation

- Potentially severe under 18 USC. § 1512.

- The statute reaches intimidation, threats, corrupt persuasion, misleading conduct, and certain forms of harassment intended to affect testimony or communication with law enforcement.

- Lawful advice to testify truthfully is expressly different from prohibited conduct.

# Most important evidentiary distinction

- The strongest case for an actual legal violation would require evidence connecting a particular person to knowledge + duty/authority + affirmative act or legally significant omission + required intent + a specific proceeding/evidence/victim affected.

- The fact that a person:

  - attended an interview,

  - represented Maxwell,

  - worked for DOJ/FBI,

  - supervised prosecutors,

  - questioned a witness,

  - failed to pursue a particular lead,

  - or possessed a document

  does not by itself establish a cover-up, obstruction, negligence, discovery violation, or ethical violation.

- Conversely, evidence of a coordinated instruction such as "do not investigate X," "do not disclose Y," "destroy/withhold Z," "keep this witness from investigators," or "tell the court something known to be false" would materially change the analysis because it can supply evidence of knowledge, intent, and participation.

# "Legal Risk" Survives

Resigning, being terminated, retiring, changing agencies, or moving to private practice generally does not erase personal legal exposure for conduct that occurred while employed.

The important distinction is between:

- criminal liability;

- professional/bar discipline;

- civil liability;

- employment or administrative consequences;

- and whether the applicable statute of limitations has expired.

## Criminal liability

If a DOJ prosecutor, FBI employee, Marshal, or other person committed a crime while employed, leaving the government does not ordinarily immunize that person from prosecution.

Depending on the facts, potentially relevant conduct could include:

- knowingly concealing or destroying evidence;

- falsifying records;

- obstructing a federal investigation or proceeding;

- witness or victim tampering;

- making materially false statements;

- conspiracy;

- aiding or facilitating another person's obstruction.

The relevant issue is generally what the person did, what the person knew, and what the person intended at the time, rather than whether the person still works for DOJ/FBI.

Separation from employment can still matter to:

- jurisdiction;

- statute-of-limitations calculations;

- available evidence;

- whether an offense is continuing;

- sentencing;

- and mitigation.

## Bar discipline 

...survives government employment

Yes. A lawyer does not ordinarily escape professional discipline by leaving DOJ, retiring, entering private practice, or becoming inactive.

- Professional-discipline jurisdiction can extend to a formerly admitted lawyer for conduct committed before resignation, suspension, disbarment, or inactive status. The exact rule depends on the governing jurisdiction. See ABA Model Rule for Lawyer Disciplinary Enforcement 6.1. ABA Model Rule for Lawyer Disciplinary Enforcement  - Rule 6americanbar.org

Potentially relevant attorney misconduct can include:

- dishonesty;

- fraud or deceit;

- knowingly assisting another lawyer's violation;

- conduct prejudicial to the administration of justice;

- misleading a tribunal;

- discovery misconduct;

- improper witness conduct;

- obstruction-related conduct.

ABA Model Rule 8.4 identifies several categories of professional misconduct, including dishonesty, fraud, deceit, misrepresentation, and conduct prejudicial to the administration of justice. ABA Model Rule 8.4americanbar.org

Therefore, for a bar-licensed DOJ lawyer:

- criminal exposure and

- professional-discipline exposure

are separate questions.

A criminal prosecution could fail while disciplinary proceedings succeed, or disciplinary proceedings could fail while criminal exposure remains, because the elements, burdens, procedures, and jurisdictions differ.

## Leaving DOJ 

...does not retroactively legalize prior conduct

Suppose a prosecutor allegedly withheld a material document in 2021 and left DOJ in 2023.

The 2023 departure does not retroactively legalize the 2021 conduct.

The relevant questions remain:

- What did the prosecutor know in 2021?

- What legal obligation existed?

- What documents or evidence were possessed?

- Was the material actually discoverable?

- Was it material?

- Was the omission intentional?

- Was there an official proceeding?

- Was there an effort to impede that proceeding?

- Did the person subsequently conceal or misrepresent what happened?

## Supervisory status 

...does not automatically create personal liability

- This is especially important for senior officials such as Todd Blanche or other DOJ leadership.

- Leaving office does not necessarily eliminate exposure for conduct a person personally:

  - ordered;

  - authorized;

  - participated in;

  - knowingly ratified;

  - or deliberately failed to correct when the person had a legally relevant duty.

- But supervisory title alone is not enough.

- Personal liability generally requires a legally sufficient connection between the individual and the underlying conduct.

- Thus:

  - "X was the supervisor" is not by itself proof of misconduct.

  - "X knew of the unlawful conduct, had authority over it, approved it, directed subordinates to continue it, and participated in concealing it" presents a materially different legal question.

## Discovery violations

- For government prosecutors, departure from DOJ does not erase potential responsibility for prior discovery conduct.

- Relevant federal disclosure authorities include:

  - Brady;

  - Giglio;

  - Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 16;

  - Rule 26.2;

  - the Jencks Act;

  - DOJ discovery policy.

- DOJ guidance identifies these authorities as important components of federal criminal discovery and states that prosecutors have responsibility for disclosure decisions. DOJ  - Guidance for Prosecutors Regarding Criminal Discoveryjustice.gov

- Knowing suppression of material exculpatory or impeachment evidence can create:

  - constitutional consequences;

  - sanctions;

  - appellate consequences;

  - professional discipline;

  - internal DOJ consequences;

  - and, if the additional statutory elements are satisfied, potential criminal exposure.

- A good-faith discovery dispute is materially different from intentional suppression.

- Likewise, an inadvertent discovery error is materially different from deliberately withholding evidence after recognizing a disclosure obligation.

## Obstruction

- Leaving employment does not ordinarily eliminate potential obstruction liability for conduct committed while employed.

- Depending on the facts and proceeding, potentially relevant federal statutes include:

  - 18 USC. § 1512  - witness, victim, evidence, and proceeding-related obstruction;

  - 18 USC. § 1503  - obstruction involving judicial proceedings;

  - 18 USC. § 1505  - obstruction involving federal agency or congressional proceedings;

  - 18 USC. § 1519  - destruction, alteration, or concealment of records in federal investigations or matters.

- The exact statute matters.

- Intent matters enormously.

- Negligence, poor judgment, an unsuccessful investigation, or a debatable prosecutorial decision should not automatically be characterized as criminal obstruction.

- Intentional concealment, destruction, falsification, witness interference, or misleading conduct undertaken with the legally required obstructive intent presents a substantially different situation.

## Cover-up

- A "cover-up" is not necessarily a standalone criminal offense.

- The legal question is what conduct constitutes the alleged cover-up.

- Potentially serious conduct could include:

  - destroying evidence;

  - concealing evidence;

  - falsifying records;

  - instructing others to conceal evidence;

  - misleading investigators;

  - misleading a court;

  - coordinating false accounts;

  - intimidating or corruptly influencing witnesses;

  - concealing an obstruction offense.

- A person who merely learns about someone else's misconduct is not automatically criminally liable.

- Exposure becomes substantially stronger where there is evidence of:

  - knowledge;

  - participation;

  - intent;

  - authority;

  - affirmative concealment;

  - or a legally significant duty to act.

## Failure to investigate

- Failure to investigate is not automatically a federal crime.

- Prosecutors have substantial investigative and charging discretion.

- The legal analysis changes if the alleged failure involved:

  - deliberate avoidance of known evidence;

  - improper discrimination or retaliation;

  - deliberate suppression of a material witness;

  - intentional avoidance of discoverable information;

  - concealment of an investigation;

  - or participation in a larger obstruction scheme.

- The distinction is therefore between:

  - reasonable prosecutorial discretion;

  - negligence or incompetence;

  - deliberate disregard of a legal obligation;

  - and intentional obstruction.

## Negligence involving victim testimony

- Ordinary negligence does not automatically become obstruction or a federal crime.

- Potential consequences can nevertheless include:

  - DOJ administrative discipline;

  - professional discipline;

  - court remedies;

  - victim-rights proceedings;

  - case-related consequences.

- The Crime Victims' Rights Act provides rights including reasonable protection, notice, a reasonable opportunity to confer with the government attorney, fairness and dignity, privacy, and reasonable access to proceedings. Federal personnel involved in detecting, investigating, or prosecuting crime must make best efforts to accord those rights. 18 USC. § 3771  - Crime Victims' Rights Actlaw.cornell.edu

- A failure to investigate a victim's testimony can therefore raise significant issues without necessarily constituting a crime.

- If the evidence instead showed that a prosecutor knew the testimony was material and deliberately prevented investigators from pursuing it in order to suppress evidence, the analysis could move toward obstruction, discovery misconduct, professional misconduct, or other legal theories depending on the facts.

## Witness or victim manipulation

- Potentially severe exposure can arise from:

  - intimidation;

  - threats;

  - corrupt persuasion;

  - misleading conduct;

  - retaliation;

  - or other prohibited interference with a witness or victim.

- 18 USC. § 1512 is particularly important in this area. 18 USC. § 1512  - Witness, Victim, and Evidence-Related Offenseslaw.cornell.edu

- Lawful witness preparation is not equivalent to witness tampering.

- Advising a witness to tell the truth is fundamentally different from knowingly attempting to cause false testimony or prevent truthful communication with investigators.

## Evidence destruction or concealment

- This can be more serious than simply making an incorrect discovery determination.

- 18 USC. § 1519 can apply to knowingly altering, destroying, mutilating, concealing, or falsifying records with the requisite intent in connection with federal investigations or matters.

- The statute provides for imprisonment of up to 20 years in qualifying circumstances. 18 USC. § 1519  - Destruction, Alteration, or Falsification of Recordslaw.cornell.edu

- The critical factual distinction is:

  - accidental loss or ordinary records-management error;

  - versus knowing concealment/destruction undertaken with prohibited intent.

## False statements

- Depending on the circumstances, 18 USC. § 1001 can apply to knowingly and willfully making materially false statements in matters within federal jurisdiction.

- Perjury statutes can apply to qualifying false statements made under oath.

- A mistake, misunderstanding, faulty memory, or immaterial inconsistency is not automatically perjury or a § 1001 violation.

  - criminal liability.

- The distinction between legitimate witness preparation and knowingly procuring false testimony is critical.

## Subsequent conduct 

...can create additional exposure

- Leaving the employer does not merely preserve the old risk.

- A former employee can potentially create new legal exposure after departure by:

  - destroying remaining evidence;

  - instructing former colleagues to conceal evidence;

  - lying to investigators;

  - submitting a false declaration;

  - misleading a court;

  - concealing relevant documents;

  - attempting to influence witnesses;

  - coordinating false accounts.

- Consequently, later conduct should be analyzed separately from the original alleged misconduct.

## Statutes of limitations 

...are an important qualification

- "Legal risk survives separation" does not mean "the person can be prosecuted or disciplined forever."

- Criminal offenses generally have applicable statutes of limitation, subject to statutory exceptions.

- Bar disciplinary proceedings have their own jurisdictional and procedural rules.

- Civil claims have separate:

  - limitations periods;

  - accrual rules;

  - immunity doctrines;

  - jurisdictional requirements.

- Therefore the legally correct question is:

Did the person's potential liability survive separation, and is the claim or proceeding still timely and legally actionable?

## Best framework 

...for the EFTA analysis

- For each person, evaluate:

  - conduct date;

  - role at the time;

  - actual legal duty;

  - actual authority;

  - documents/evidence possessed;

  - knowledge;

  - action or omission;

  - intent;

  - materiality;

  - effect on a proceeding or investigation;

  - effect on victims/witnesses;

  - subsequent concealment or correction;

  - current employment/bar status;

  - applicable statute of limitations.

- Current employer is therefore not the controlling variable.

- Someone who was a DOJ prosecutor when an alleged discovery violation occurred does not become immune because that person later became a private lawyer.

- Conversely, someone who joined DOJ later should not automatically inherit liability for conduct that occurred before that person became involved.

## Bottom line

### Employment liability

- may end when employment ends.

### Criminal liability

- generally does not disappear merely because employment ends.

### Bar liability

- generally does not disappear merely because a lawyer leaves government service.

### Civil liability

- may survive, subject to immunity, limitations, jurisdiction, and other doctrines.

### Supervisory liability

- requires a personal factual/legal connection; title alone is insufficient.

### Later concealment

- can potentially create additional liability after separation.

### Negligence

- is not automatically obstruction or a crime.

### Intentional suppression

...concealment, falsification, witness interference, or obstruction

- can remain legally significant after the person leaves the employer.

### Statutes of limitations and jurisdiction

- ultimately determine whether particular exposure remains actionable.

- Accordingly, for the EFTA personnel review, "left DOJ" or "left the prosecution team" should be treated as a change in employment status, not as an exonerating fact. The more important fields are what happened, when it happened, what the person knew, what duty or authority existed, what the person did or failed to do, what intent can be proven, and whether the conduct remained legally actionable.
