## Article

The Resolution of Inherent Contempt is a constitutional authority that allows the House of Representatives to act as its own judicial body to enforce subpoenas or laws without the assistance of the executive or judicial branches.

Unlike "criminal contempt," which requires the Department of Justice to prosecute its own officials, or "civil contempt," which relies on federal courts, inherent contempt allows the House to directly detain or fine individuals.

## Epstein Files Transparency Act

In the context of the Epstein Files Transparency Act (H.R. 4405), lawmakers are utilizing this power because the DOJ failed to meet the December 19, 2025, deadline for the full release of unredacted files.

By passing a resolution of inherent contempt, the House can bypass the DOJ's refusal to prosecute its own leadership and apply direct pressure - specifically through daily fines - until the legal requirements of the Act are met.

## Prior Uses

### 1795

The House ordered the arrest of Robert Randall and Charles Whitney for attempting to bribe members of Congress.

Randall was brought to the "bar of the House," tried, and detained for one week.

### 1800

The Senate exercised this power against William Duane, a newspaper editor who published "libelous" articles about the Senate.

After refusing to appear, he was ordered into custody.

### 1812

The House held Nathaniel Rounsavell in contempt for refusing to answer questions regarding a leak of secret session information.

He was detained until he agreed to testify.

### 1821

In the case of Anderson v. Dunn, the Supreme Court formally upheld the House's power to arrest and detain John Anderson for contempt, ruling the power was "inherent" and necessary for the legislature to function.

### 1832

The House arrested and tried Sam Houston for assaulting Representative William Stanbery.

Houston was brought before the bar of the House, found guilty of contempt, and reprimanded.

### 1917

In Marshall v. Gordon, the Supreme Court clarified that while the House could use inherent contempt to preserve its legislative ability, the power was limited to "the least possible power adequate to the end proposed."

### 1934

The Senate used inherent contempt to arrest and trial William P. MacCracken Jr., a former government official, for destroying documents related to an investigation.

This was the last time a full "trial at the bar" resulted in physical detention.

## Step by Step Timeline

The Resolution of Inherent Contempt has emerged as the primary enforcement mechanism for the Epstein Files Transparency Act (Public Law 119-38).

Because the Department of Justice (DOJ) failed to meet the statutory deadline for a full, unredacted release of the investigative files, lawmakers are bypassing standard judicial referrals and utilizing the House’s own constitutional authority to compel compliance.

### November 18, 2025

The House of Representatives passed H.R. 4405, the Epstein Files Transparency Act, by a bipartisan vote of 427–1.

### November 19, 2025

The Senate passed the bill by unanimous consent, and the President signed it into law later that day, triggering a 30-day mandatory clock for the Department of Justice to release all unclassified records.

### December 19, 2025

The legal deadline for the DOJ to release the files expired; the agency released a large volume of documents that lawmakers criticized for being heavily redacted, including the blacking out of names of government officials.

### December 21, 2025

Representatives Thomas Massie and Ro Khanna announced on national television that they were drafting a Resolution of Inherent Contempt to fine Attorney General Pam Bondi personally for failing to comply with the law.

### December 29, 2025

Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer introduced a separate resolution to force a Senate-led lawsuit against the DOJ, while House members finalized the "daily fine" structure for the Inherent Contempt resolution.

### January 5, 2026

Congress is scheduled to return from winter recess, marking the first opportunity for the Resolution of Inherent Contempt to be formally introduced on the House floor.

### January 13, 2026

The House Oversight Committee has scheduled high-profile depositions for individuals linked to the investigation, with the intent to use testimony to challenge the DOJ’s specific redaction justifications.

### February 2026

Should the resolution pass in January, February marks the beginning of the "enforcement phase" where the House Sergeant-at-Arms would be authorized to collect fines or, theoretically, take officials into custody until the documents are produced.

## Hypothetical Legal Strategy

The following is a hypothetical, legally neutral framework for evaluating preserved evidence and deciding whether federal, state, civil, or coordinated proceedings could be viable.

It is not a recommendation to prosecute any named person, and preserved material is not necessarily admissible evidence.

The critical distinction is between evidence that suggests wrongdoing and admissible evidence that proves every element of a particular offense beyond a reasonable doubt.

Because the underlying article concerns politically significant matters and contains assertions about congressional and executive conduct, the framework below relies on current federal legal authorities rather than assuming that every factual assertion in the article is established.

### 1. Start with an evidence-preservation and jurisdiction assessment

The first step should be to identify exactly what evidence exists, who possesses it, how it was obtained, and which jurisdiction has authority over the suspected conduct.

Investigators should create an evidence inventory covering:

- Original documents and electronically stored information.

- Emails, text messages, messaging-platform records, calendars, contact records, call records, and other communications.

- Financial records, bank records, corporate records, invoices, transfers, tax records, and ownership information.

- Audio, video, photographs, surveillance material, metadata, and location information where lawfully obtained.

- Government records, subpoenas, deposition transcripts, testimony, investigative reports, and records of official proceedings.

- Witness statements and contemporaneous notes.

- Evidence concerning the creation, modification, deletion, concealment, or destruction of other evidence.

The preservation process should maintain chain of custody and document who collected each item, when it was collected, where it was stored, how it was preserved, and whether it was altered during forensic examination.

Authentication is a separate issue from preservation.

Federal Rule of Evidence 901 generally requires evidence sufficient to support a finding that an item is what its proponent claims it is.

For documents, recordings, and photographs, the prosecution must also consider the Federal Rules of Evidence governing originals and duplicates.

Rule 1002 generally addresses the requirement of an original when the contents of a writing, recording, or photograph are being proved, subject to the rules' exceptions.

### 2. Build a factual chronology before selecting charges

Investigators should construct a chronology without initially assuming that a crime occurred.

For each significant event, identify:

- What happened.

- Who participated.

- What each person knew at the time.

- What each person intended.

- What communications occurred before and after the event.

- What action followed the communication.

- Whether the conduct affected money, property, testimony, evidence, a government proceeding, or another legally protected interest.

- Whether the conduct occurred within federal jurisdiction, state jurisdiction, or both.

This prevents investigators from starting with a desired charge and attempting to force ambiguous evidence into its elements.

Federal prosecutors are instructed to consider whether probable cause exists, whether further investigation is warranted, and whether admissible evidence will probably be sufficient to obtain and sustain a conviction.

DOJ guidance expressly distinguishes probable cause from the substantially higher evidentiary showing required for conviction.

### 3. Separate the possible legal theories

The same factual conduct can potentially implicate different legal theories, but each theory has independent elements.

Possible federal categories could include, depending on the actual evidence:

- Conspiracy to commit a federal offense or to defraud the United States under 18 USC. 371.

- Obstruction-related offenses.

- Witness or victim tampering.

- Destruction, alteration, or concealment of evidence.

- Mail fraud or wire fraud if the statutory elements are actually satisfied.

- False statements or perjury where the required elements and jurisdictional prerequisites exist.

Section 371, for example, requires an agreement involving two or more persons to commit an offense against the United States or to defraud the United States, plus an overt act by one or more conspirators in furtherance of the conspiracy.

Wire fraud requires considerably more than proof that communications occurred.

The statute requires a qualifying scheme or artifice and transmission of specified communications in interstate or foreign commerce for the purpose of executing that scheme.

Mail fraud similarly requires the statutory elements of a qualifying fraudulent scheme and use of the mail or an authorized carrier in the manner specified by the statute.

### 4. Analyze a possible two-or-more-person coordinated effort separately

If the evidence suggests that two or more people coordinated their conduct, investigators should not simply infer a conspiracy from association, friendship, employment, simultaneous conduct, or knowledge of one another.

The central questions should be:

- Did the individuals actually reach an agreement?

- What was the object of the agreement?

- Was the object unlawful under the particular conspiracy statute being considered?

- Did each defendant knowingly and voluntarily join the agreement?

- What acts did each participant personally undertake?

- What communications demonstrate agreement or shared intent?

- What evidence distinguishes actual agreement from independent conduct?

- Was there an overt act if the applicable conspiracy statute requires one?

Federal conspiracy prosecutions require proof of the applicable statutory elements beyond a reasonable doubt.

A DOJ model instruction for a federal conspiracy offense describes the essential concepts as an unlawful agreement, knowing and willful membership, and the required overt act and connection to the conspiracy's objective.

Accordingly, a particularly important evidentiary distinction would be between evidence showing "Person A knew what Person B was doing" and evidence showing "Person A knowingly agreed with Person B to accomplish the unlawful objective."

### 5. Gather Evidence that could establish coordinated intent

Potential evidence could include:

- Direct communications discussing the intended objective.

- Messages assigning tasks to particular participants.

- Emails or texts discussing what each participant should do.

- Communications occurring immediately before or after important acts.

- Shared documents or drafts.

- Records showing coordinated financial transactions.

- Calendar records demonstrating coordinated meetings.

- Evidence that one person's conduct was dependent upon or responsive to another person's instructions.

- Statements by participants acknowledging the arrangement.

- Witness testimony describing meetings or conversations.

- Evidence of deliberate concealment that is probative of consciousness of guilt, subject to applicable evidentiary limitations.

- Multiple independent pieces of circumstantial evidence that converge on the same explanation.

The strongest conspiracy case would ordinarily seek several independent evidentiary paths rather than depending upon a single ambiguous communication.

### 6. Distinguish legitimate coordination from criminal conspiracy

Coordination alone is not criminal.

People may lawfully communicate, share lawyers, respond to the same government investigation, coordinate public statements, conduct business together, or pursue mutually compatible lawful objectives.

Therefore, the prosecution would have to identify the specific unlawful objective and prove the required mental state.

The evidence should establish more than:

- Mere association.

- Mere knowledge.

- Mere presence.

- Mere employment.

- Mere political agreement.

- Mere participation in a lawful activity.

- Mere communication with another suspect.

The required proof depends on the particular statute charged.

### 7. Preserve evidence through lawful investigative processes

Once investigators identify potentially relevant evidence, they should preserve it through appropriate legal mechanisms.

Depending on the circumstances, this could include:

- Preservation requests.

- Subpoenas.

- Search warrants.

- Grand-jury subpoenas.

- Court orders.

- Consensual interviews.

- Voluntary production.

- Financial-record requests authorized by applicable law.

- Digital forensic imaging.

- Authentication testimony or certifications.

- Expert examination.

The objective should be preservation of the original evidentiary state rather than modification or unnecessary duplication.

For regularly conducted business records, Federal Rule of Evidence 803(6) provides a potential hearsay exception when its specified requirements are satisfied, including appropriate testimony or certification concerning how the records were created and maintained.

### 8. Identify the appropriate federal investigative agencies

The investigative agency should depend upon the suspected offense rather than political identity or status.

Potential federal participants could include:

- Federal Bureau of Investigation personnel for qualifying federal criminal investigations.

- Internal Revenue Service Criminal Investigation for qualifying federal tax offenses.

- Postal Inspection Service for qualifying offenses involving the mail.

- Financial Crimes Enforcement Network or other Treasury components where their statutory authorities are relevant.

- Inspectors General where alleged misconduct involves a federal department or agency within an Inspector General's jurisdiction.

- Other federal investigative agencies possessing specific statutory jurisdiction.

The investigating agency should preserve evidence and develop probable cause while prosecutors independently evaluate legal sufficiency.

### 9. Identify the necessary prosecutors

A federal prosecution would ordinarily involve:

- An Assistant United States Attorney or Department of Justice prosecutor assigned to the matter.

- A supervisory federal prosecutor.

- Specialized Criminal Division attorneys where the subject matter requires specialized authority or approval.

- Potentially an appropriate United States Attorney's Office if venue lies within a particular federal district.

- DOJ leadership or specialized components when Department policies require consultation or approval.

The prosecutor's function is not simply to assemble damaging material.

The prosecutor must determine whether the evidence is legally admissible and sufficient to prove every charged element.

DOJ's Principles of Federal Prosecution state that federal prosecution should generally be considered when the conduct constitutes a federal offense, admissible evidence will probably be sufficient to obtain and sustain a conviction, and a substantial federal interest would be served, subject to the stated alternatives and considerations.

### 10. Identify potential witnesses

Witnesses should be categorized according to what element they can establish.

Potential categories include:

- Victims who can establish the harm or conduct directly experienced.

- Eyewitnesses who personally observed relevant conduct.

- Participants who can describe an agreement or meeting.

- Cooperating witnesses who can establish communications or intent.

- Records custodians who can authenticate records.

- Digital forensic specialists who can establish how electronic evidence was acquired and analyzed.

- Financial investigators who can explain transactions.

- Government officials who can establish the existence and status of an official proceeding.

- Expert witnesses when specialized knowledge is necessary.

- Investigating agents who can explain collection and investigative procedures.

A witness should not be treated as necessary merely because the witness has a compelling story.

The relevant question is what admissible testimony that witness can provide concerning a specific element.

### 11. Treat victims differently from witnesses

A victim may be both a victim and a witness, but the concepts are distinct.

Victim evidence could establish:

- What conduct occurred.

- What harm resulted.

- Whether the defendant's conduct affected the victim.

- Communications made to the victim.

- Financial or physical consequences.

- Relevant chronology.

But victim testimony does not automatically establish criminal intent or conspiracy.

Those elements may require additional evidence.

Federal law also contains specific protections concerning witness and victim tampering.

Section 1512 prohibits specified forms of intimidation, threats, misleading conduct, corrupt persuasion, and other conduct intended to affect testimony, records, or communication with federal law enforcement.

### 12. Evaluate possible obstruction separately

If evidence shows that someone attempted to destroy, conceal, alter, or otherwise interfere with evidence or a proceeding, investigators should analyze the exact obstruction statute rather than treating "obstruction" as a generic offense.

For example, 18 USC. 1512(c) addresses specified corrupt conduct involving alteration, destruction, mutilation, concealment of records or other objects and obstruction or impediment of official proceedings.

Section 1505 separately addresses obstruction involving pending proceedings before Congress and federal departments and agencies under circumstances covered by that statute.

This distinction matters because the government must prove the particular statutory elements applicable to the conduct.

### 13. Develop the state-law track independently

A state investigation should not simply copy the federal theory.

The appropriate state prosecutor could be:

- A county District Attorney.

- A state Attorney General.

- A specialized state prosecutor or bureau where state law provides one.

- A special prosecutor where a conflict of interest exists.

The relevant state agency could include:

- State police.

- Local police.

- State financial or regulatory investigators.

- State Inspector General personnel.

The state prosecutor would need to identify a specific state offense, establish state territorial or personal jurisdiction, preserve admissible evidence, and satisfy the applicable state charging and trial standards.

If New York were the relevant state, for example, the actual charging analysis would have to begin with New York statutes and New York criminal procedure rather than assuming that federal conspiracy or obstruction provisions apply identically at the state level.

### 14. Develop the civil track separately

A civil proceeding has a different purpose and different burdens of proof.

Potential civil plaintiffs could include:

- Individual victims.

- Estates.

- Businesses.

- Government entities.

- Regulatory authorities.

- Other parties possessing an applicable statutory or common-law cause of action.

Potential civil theories would depend entirely upon the facts and could include:

- Fraud.

- Breach of fiduciary duty.

- Unjust enrichment.

- Negligence.

- Civil statutory violations.

- Recovery of improperly obtained property.

- Injunctive relief.

A civil case should not be used merely as a substitute for a criminal prosecution.

It requires an independently viable cause of action and appropriate standing, jurisdiction, and remedies.

### 15. Grand jury and charging stage

For a federal felony prosecution, prosecutors may present evidence to a federal grand jury seeking an indictment.

The indictment must adequately identify the essential facts and elements of the offenses charged.

DOJ materials emphasize that an essential element cannot simply be supplied later by prosecutors or the court.

At this stage, the government does not yet have to prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt to the grand jury in the same manner required at trial.

The probable-cause threshold is materially different from the trial standard.

The indictment therefore should be constructed around provable statutory elements rather than broad allegations of misconduct.

### 16. Pretrial litigation

After charging, prosecutors should expect defense litigation concerning:

- Suppression of evidence.

- Search and seizure.

- Privilege.

- Authentication.

- Hearsay.

- Expert testimony.

- Discovery.

- Brady and Giglio material.

- Witness credibility.

- Constitutional challenges.

- Venue.

- Statute of limitations.

- Multiplicity or duplicity.

- Confrontation Clause issues.

- Selective or vindictive prosecution claims where legally cognizable.

The prosecution should test its own evidence against the strongest foreseeable defense arguments before trial.

### 17. Trial proof for each defendant

The government should construct an element-by-element proof chart for each defendant.

For every count, identify:

- Element 1.

- Evidence proving Element 1.

- Element 2.

- Evidence proving Element 2.

- Required mental state.

- Evidence proving the mental state.

- Evidence establishing jurisdiction.

- Authentication foundation.

- Potential hearsay issue.

- Anticipated defense.

- Rebuttal evidence.

- Witness responsible for each evidentiary foundation.

The final question should be:

> Could a rational jury, after considering only admissible evidence, find every element beyond a reasonable doubt?

That is fundamentally different from asking whether the total evidence looks suspicious or whether the defendant probably did something wrong.

### 18. What would have to be proved in a coordinated-effort case

For a hypothetical federal conspiracy under 18 USC. 371, the prosecution would generally need evidence establishing:

- Two or more persons entered an agreement of the type covered by the statute.

- The defendant knowingly and voluntarily participated in that agreement.

- The agreement had the unlawful objective alleged in the indictment.

- At least one conspirator committed the required overt act.

- The overt act furthered the conspiracy's objective.

- The government's evidence establishes the applicable elements beyond a reasonable doubt.

Section 371 itself establishes the statutory framework, while jury instructions and controlling appellate precedent determine the precise formulation applicable in a particular prosecution.

### 19. Communications and common intent

Communications can be particularly important because they may provide direct evidence of knowledge, intent, agreement, or assignment of tasks.

For example, a hypothetical evidentiary sequence might contain:

- Communication A: Person 1 proposes an unlawful objective.

- Communication B: Person 2 agrees to participate.

- Communication C: Person 1 assigns Person 2 a particular task.

- Financial record: Person 2 performs an act consistent with the assignment.

- Communication D: Person 2 reports completion.

- Independent witness: The assigned act actually occurred.

- Subsequent communication: The participants discuss concealing the conduct.

No single item necessarily proves the entire case.

Together, authenticated communications, independent corroboration, conduct, financial records, and witness testimony could potentially establish a coherent evidentiary chain.

Whether that chain is legally sufficient would depend on the exact statute and facts.

### 20. Possible impact of convictions

The effects of convictions should be described as legal consequences rather than assumed political consequences.

Depending upon the offenses and defendants, convictions could result in:

- Imprisonment.

- Fines.

- Restitution where legally authorized.

- Forfeiture.

- Probation or supervised release.

- Civil liability.

- Professional or licensing consequences.

- Immigration consequences for some noncitizens.

- Restrictions arising from particular statutes.

- Additional investigative cooperation from convicted or cooperating participants.

A conspiracy conviction can also have evidentiary consequences in subsequent proceedings because a proven conspiracy may establish relationships and conduct relevant to other defendants, although the admissibility of particular evidence remains governed by the applicable evidence rules.

DOJ's prosecution guidance specifically identifies deterrence, culpability, victims' interests, and probable sentencing consequences as considerations in prosecutorial decision-making.

### 21. Possible impact of convictions on additional investigations

A conviction of one participant could produce additional evidence through:

- Cooperation agreements.

- Plea agreements.

- Sentencing proceedings.

- Forfeiture proceedings.

- Production of previously unknown records.

- Identification of additional participants.

- Testimony concerning communications and meetings.

- Identification of financial transactions.

That does not mean a conviction automatically proves another person's guilt.

Each additional defendant requires an individualized evidentiary showing.

DOJ guidance expressly calls for individualized charging decisions and recognizes a defendant's willingness to cooperate in investigating or prosecuting others as one consideration in federal prosecutorial decision-making.

### 22. A practical zero-to-prosecution sequence

A legally disciplined hypothetical sequence would therefore be:

- Step 1: Preserve all potentially relevant evidence.

- Step 2: Establish provenance and chain of custody.

- Step 3: Separate allegations from independently corroborated facts.

- Step 4: Construct a chronology.

- Step 5: Identify possible federal and state jurisdictions.

- Step 6: Identify possible offenses without prematurely selecting charges.

- Step 7: Map each item of evidence to a specific statutory element.

- Step 8: Identify missing evidence.

- Step 9: Obtain additional evidence through lawful investigative procedures.

- Step 10: Interview witnesses and evaluate credibility.

- Step 11: Authenticate electronic, documentary, financial, audio, and video evidence.

- Step 12: Determine whether probable cause exists.

- Step 13: Determine whether admissible evidence is likely to sustain a conviction.

- Step 14: Obtain required supervisory and prosecutorial approvals.

- Step 15: Present appropriate federal charges to a grand jury when required.

- Step 16: File charges in the appropriate jurisdiction.

- Step 17: Conduct discovery and disclose constitutionally required material.

- Step 18: Litigate suppression, evidentiary, constitutional, and procedural issues.

- Step 19: Protect victims and witnesses from intimidation or retaliation.

- Step 20: Proceed to trial or a lawful negotiated disposition.

- Step 21: At trial, prove each element beyond a reasonable doubt.

- Step 22: If convicted, proceed to sentencing, restitution, forfeiture, and other legally authorized consequences.

### 23. What would make the coordinated-effort theory substantially stronger

The hypothetical case becomes substantially more concrete if investigators can establish an evidentiary chain such as:

- Direct evidence of an agreement.

- Independent corroboration of the agreement.

- Evidence establishing each defendant's knowing participation.

- Evidence showing a common unlawful objective.

- Specific acts by individual participants in furtherance of that objective.

- Contemporaneous communications matching the later conduct.

- Financial or documentary records independently corroborating the communications.

- Witnesses with personal knowledge.

- Authentic and lawfully obtained electronic evidence.

- Evidence addressing innocent explanations for the same conduct.

The last point is important.

A prosecution must account for plausible innocent interpretations of ambiguous evidence.

The issue is not whether investigators can construct a suspicious narrative, but whether admissible evidence permits proof of the charged elements beyond a reasonable doubt.

### 24. Important limitation concerning the supplied article

The supplied article should not itself be treated as evidence of criminal conduct.

Assertions in an article, congressional statements, allegations, or references to investigative files may provide investigative leads, but prosecutors would still have to establish the underlying facts through admissible evidence.

Likewise, the existence of a common communication strategy, legal strategy, political objective, or public-relations strategy would not by itself establish a criminal conspiracy.

The prosecution would have to prove the specific unlawful agreement and mental state required by the statute charged.

The most legally defensible hypothetical strategy is therefore not "build a case against particular people," but "identify provable offenses, preserve potentially exculpatory and inculpatory evidence, independently test competing explanations, and prosecute only where the evidence satisfies the applicable legal standards."
