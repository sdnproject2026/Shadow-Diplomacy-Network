# NM AG Files Fed Civil Lawsuit

## Freeze $100 Million Estate Funds

### Status as of October 25, 2026

New Mexico Attorney General Raúl Torrez filed a federal civil lawsuit against Jeffrey Epstein accomplices:

- personal attorney Darren Indyke

- accountant Richard Kahn

accusing them of financing and concealing Epstein's sex trafficking operation. 

### Law Enforcement Action

Filed in the US District Court for the District of New Mexico, the lawsuit marks the first major law enforcement action taken directly against either adviser.

## Key Details

### The Accusations

- The civil complaint alleges that Indyke and Kahn intentionally facilitated, financed, and covered up decades of sexual abuse of girls and young women. 

- This includes abuse alleged to have taken place at Epstein's sprawling, 26,700-square-foot Zorro Ranch located south of Santa Fe, New Mexico.

### Targeting the Estate

- Indyke and Kahn serve as the co-executors of Epstein’s private trust and estate, which is currently valued at more than $100 million. 

- The lawsuit explicitly seeks to freeze and block any further payments, distributions, or profits from reaching the two men while the litigation is ongoing.

### Damages for Survivors

- Attorney General Torrez stated that any remaining funds from the estate should be stripped from the advisers and instead allocated as compensatory and punitive damages, restitution, and disgorgement to the survivors of Epstein's abuse.

### The Defense

- Both Indyke and Kahn have previously denied any knowledge of or participation in Epstein’s trafficking operation, stating in congressional testimony earlier this year that they were unaware of the ongoing abuse.

## Status of Broader Investigations

This federal civil lawsuit is proceeding alongside a separate criminal investigation into activities at Zorro Ranch. New Mexico state authorities officially reopened that criminal probe in February, though no criminal charges have been filed against the advisers as of the reported date.

### 2025 Epstein Files Transparency Act

Additionally, New Mexico is actively suing the US Department of Justice to force the release of completely unredacted federal investigative files on Epstein, accusing the federal government of withholding key records mandated by the 2025 Epstein Files Transparency Act.

# Trafficking Or Racketeering Enterprise

The key distinction in these four questions is between evidence actually documented by investigators, allegations made in a civil complaint, and legal arguments or outcomes that remain unverified. 

A lawsuit can describe suspicious financial transactions or allege concealment, but that does not automatically establish criminal participation in a trafficking or racketeering enterprise.

I’ll separate those categories, particularly for the reported March 2026 ranch search and the dispute over unredacted federal records.

## 1. What concrete evidence from the March 2026 Zorro Ranch search linked Indyke and Kahn to concealment?

The key qualification is that publicly available reporting has not established that the March 2026 physical search uncovered specific evidence directly linking Darren Indyke and Richard Kahn to a concealment scheme.

New Mexico authorities searched Zorro Ranch beginning March 9, 2026, as part of a reopened criminal investigation. Initial reporting did not disclose the search's findings or identify particular evidence seized from the property. 

The state later said federal investigative records were important to identifying witnesses, victims, and possible additional criminal conduct.

The stronger publicly reported allegations linking Indyke and Kahn come from the separate civil complaint filed October 21, 2026, which cites financial records and corporate activities.

### Examples include

- Payments to women and girls: The complaint identifies more than $3 million in payments from Epstein-related accounts to women and girls between 2003 and 2013 that allegedly lacked a discernible legitimate business purpose.

- Medical expenses: A 2017 email from Kahn to Epstein reportedly discussed paying the latest batch of medical expenses for girls.

- Cash withdrawals: Indyke allegedly structured cash withdrawals to avoid federal reporting requirements.

- Corporate records: Indyke allegedly handled the 2011 transfer of the ranch's title to an Epstein-controlled company. Both men held positions in Epstein-related entities and exercised financial authority.

- Payments through a nonprofit: The complaint alleges that the C.O.U.Q. Foundation, overseen by Indyke and Kahn, paid more than $30,000 to women or girls in 2012 for purposes apparently unrelated to the charity.

These allegations could be relevant to concealment if investigators establish that the transactions were knowingly used to finance, facilitate, or disguise trafficking activities. 

However, they should not be mischaracterized as physical evidence recovered during the March search.

What remains unconfirmed: Public reporting reviewed here does not establish that investigators recovered a particular ledger, computer, email archive, physical document, or other item during the ranch search that independently proves either man's participation in concealment.

## 2. How do the financial metrics distinguish legitimate services from racketeering?

There is no single dollar threshold that automatically transforms legal or accounting work into racketeering. The distinction turns on the nature of the transactions, the defendants' knowledge and intent, and whether their conduct satisfies the elements of the relevant claims.

The state's allegations rely on several indicators that, taken together, it argues go beyond ordinary professional services.

- More than $3 million in payments to women and girls (2003–2013): Recipients, timing, purpose, and supporting records may connect payments to recruitment, abuse, or victim management. 

- However, the total alone does not establish that every payment was improper or that either defendant knew its true purpose.

- At least 140 bank accounts associated with Epstein and his businesses by 2005: 

- Multiple accounts and intercompany transfers may reveal how money was routed and who controlled it. 

- Maintaining multiple accounts is not inherently unlawful.

- More than $6 million in cash withdrawals (2002–2013): Withdrawal patterns may help trace cash used for trafficking-related activities. 

- The total does not establish that all withdrawals were connected to crimes.

- Alleged repeated withdrawals near $10,000: A pattern of transactions deliberately structured to evade applicable reporting requirements may indicate concealment. 

- Withdrawals below $10,000 are not automatically illegal; the surrounding circumstances and intent matter.

- Payments for medical expenses, rent, tuition, and legal costs: Context may establish whether payments supported victims, recruiters, or other participants in the alleged enterprise. 

- Paying such expenses is not inherently criminal.

- More than $16 million received by Indyke and more than $10 million by Kahn (2011–2019): Compensation, timing, services performed, and related transactions can help establish financial relationships and potential benefits. 

- Large professional fees alone do not prove criminal involvement.

The complaint also describes Indyke's and Kahn's roles as officers, directors, account managers, and authorized signatories for Epstein-related entities.

### What would make the distinction legally meaningful?

Investigators would need evidence supporting a more specific chain of reasoning:

1. Knowledge: The adviser knew, or possessed the legally required level of knowledge, that particular transactions were connected to trafficking or other unlawful activity.

2. Intentional conduct: The adviser knowingly approved, arranged, structured, or concealed transactions for an unlawful purpose, rather than merely performing routine professional work.

3. Connection to the alleged enterprise: The conduct helped advance or conceal the enterprise, as required by the particular legal claim.

4. Corroboration: Bank records, communications, testimony, transaction instructions, and evidence concerning recipients or payment purposes support the allegation.

For example, a properly documented legal fee for legitimate estate work is different from a payment knowingly routed through a corporate entity to conceal the proceeds of a crime. 

The transaction's context and evidence of intent matter more than its size alone.

The New Mexico complaint asserts violations of the federal Trafficking Victims Protection Act and the New Mexico Racketeering Act. 

These remain allegations to be tested in court, not findings that either defendant has committed those offenses.

## 3. What legal arguments is the DOJ using to withhold unredacted files?

The publicly reported position of the US Department of Justice centers on victim privacy, existing judicial protective orders, and the scope of the state's request.

### A. Victim-identifying information must remain protected

The DOJ has argued that the Epstein Files Transparency Act does not require unrestricted disclosure of victim-identifying information and that protective orders entered in federal proceedings prohibit disclosure of such information without an adequate legal basis.

The department has characterized New Mexico's request as overly broad, arguing that the state has not established a lawful basis for the sweeping disclosure it seeks.

This is a significant distinction: the dispute concerns access to unredacted investigative records, which may include sensitive information, rather than simply whether the public should receive redacted documents.

### B. Existing federal court orders constrain disclosure

According to the DOJ, protective orders in the Southern District of New York restrict disclosure of certain material. 

The department maintains that neither a request from another jurisdiction nor a general interest in cooperation overrides those orders or victim-privacy protections.

Whether the orders actually prevent the specific disclosures New Mexico seeks, and whether controlled access could be granted under appropriate safeguards, are legal questions for the courts.

### C. Formal agency procedures apply to state requests

New Mexico says federal officials directed it to submit formal requests under the DOJ's Touhy regulations, which govern requests for agency information or testimony.

The state says it complied but did not receive a substantive response. It argues that the DOJ's refusal or delay is unlawful under the Administrative Procedure Act and interferes with the state's sovereign authority to investigate and prosecute state crimes.

The DOJ's reported response is that Touhy procedures do not override victim privacy or binding judicial orders.

### D. The scope of the Transparency Act is disputed

New Mexico contends that the federal government is withholding records needed for its criminal investigation despite the Epstein Files Transparency Act's disclosure requirements.

The DOJ counters that the Act does not require disclosure of victim-identifying information in defiance of existing protective orders.

The state's lawsuit seeks an order requiring production of the requested records or, alternatively, a prompt and lawful reconsideration of the request.

Important qualification: The publicly reported DOJ position does not establish that every requested document is legally exempt from disclosure. Nor does it establish that the department can withhold all unredacted records indefinitely. 

The precise scope of permissible disclosure depends on the statute, applicable orders, the contents of particular records, and any judicial ruling.

## 4. Will the civil lawsuit affect the timeline or viability of criminal charges?

The civil action may provide useful evidence and investigative leads, but it does not itself determine whether criminal charges will be filed.

### Potential effects on the criminal investigation

- Evidence development: Financial records and corporate documents described in the complaint may help investigators identify transactions, witnesses, and possible links between payments and conduct at Zorro Ranch.

- Witness identification: The civil case may bring additional information into the public record or identify people who could provide evidence. 

- Investigators would still need to assess credibility, corroborate testimony, and establish admissible evidence.

- Access to records: The separate dispute with the DOJ may be more consequential to the criminal timeline than the civil lawsuit itself. 

- New Mexico has said it needs federal records to identify victims and witnesses and to determine the scope of possible crimes.

- Parallel proceedings: Civil discovery and criminal investigations follow different procedures and standards. 

- Material developed in one proceeding may be useful in the other, subject to evidentiary rules, confidentiality requirements, privileges, and restrictions on the use of compelled testimony.

### Could the civil case help establish criminal liability?

Potentially, but the standards differ.

- Burden of proof: A civil claim generally uses a lower burden of proof than a criminal prosecution. 

- Criminal prosecutors must establish each charged offense beyond a reasonable doubt.

- Criminal intent: Financial irregularities or evidence of a professional relationship with Epstein are not sufficient on their own to prove criminal intent.

- Elements of the offense: Investigators must establish the elements of specific offenses and address jurisdiction, applicable limitation periods, and the admissibility and reliability of evidence.

- Independent assessment: The civil complaint may help organize evidence and clarify allegations, but a judge's decision in that case would not automatically establish criminal guilt.

### What is known about the timeline?

As of October 25, 2026, New Mexico's criminal investigation remains ongoing. In August, Attorney General Raúl Torrez said the state needed access to federal files before it could determine whether to charge someone. The state has also acknowledged challenges arising from the passage of time, the ranch's sale in 2023, and possible jurisdictional issues.

The October civil filing does not announce a criminal charging decision or provide a confirmed timetable for one.

Bottom line: The financial allegations may strengthen investigative leads, while access to federal files, corroboration, admissible evidence, and the legal elements of individual offenses will be central to any criminal prosecution. 

Neither the civil lawsuit nor the current public record establishes that criminal charges against Indyke or Kahn are inevitable.
