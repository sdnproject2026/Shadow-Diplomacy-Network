# EFTA II

## Secured 218 Signatures

### November 11, 2026 

A bipartisan group of lawmakers in the US House of Representatives secured the 218 signatures needed on a discharge petition to force a floor vote on the Epstein Files Transparency Act II. 

### The Discharge Petition

- Introduced by Rep. (GOP) Thomas Massie (KY) alongside Democratic lawmakers, the petition reached its 218-signature threshold on Wednesday after Rep. (DEM) John Garamendi (CA) added the final signature.

- All House Democrats and four Republicans—Rep. (GOP) Thomas Massie (KY), Rep. (GOP) Brian Fitzpatrick (PA), Rep. (GOP) Nancy Mace (SC), and Rep. (GOP) Ralph Norman (SC)—signed on to bypass House leadership and force a vote. 

### Right to Sue the DOJ

- As noted in the image, the legislation allows state attorneys general, district attorneys, members of Congress, and Epstein survivors to file federal lawsuits against the US Attorney General and the Department of Justice (DOJ) for withholding, delaying, or improperly redacting Epstein-related records. 

### Expanded Record Access

- The bill would grant survivors private access to unredacted records mentioning them (such as FBI interview reports) and require the DOJ to provide state and local prosecutors with unredacted materials to pursue investigations. 

### Background

- The measure follows the original Epstein Files Transparency Act, which was signed into law in November 2025. 

- Lawmakers pushing for the follow-up bill allege that the DOJ has failed to fully comply with the 2025 law by continuing to withhold over three million documents and applying heavy redactions. 

Further coverage of the petition and upcoming vote is available via CBS News and Politico.

## Legislative Analysis

The provided text details a major procedural and legislative milestone reached on November 11, 2026, within the US House of Representatives. 

A bipartisan coalition successfully gathered 218 signatures on a discharge petition, a rare parliamentary mechanism used to bypass House leadership and force a floor vote on the Epstein Files Transparency Act II (EFTA II).

### Discharge Petition Threshold

- The discharge petition reached the required 218-signature majority after Rep. (DEM) John Garamendi (CA) added his signature on Wednesday

- The petition united all House Democrats with four key Republicans: Rep. (GOP) Thomas Massie (KY), Rep. (GOP) Brian Fitzpatrick (PA), Rep. (GOP) Nancy Mace (SC), and Rep. (GOP) Ralph Norman (SC)

### Right to Sue the DOJ

- The legislation grants legal standing to state attorneys general, district attorneys, members of Congress, and Epstein survivors to file federal lawsuits against the US Attorney General and the Department of Justice

- Lawsuits can be initiated if the Department of Justice withholds, delays, or improperly redacts records related to Jeffrey Epstein

### Access to Unredacted Records

- Epstein survivors are granted private access to unredacted records mentioning them, including FBI interview reports

- Mandatory provision of unredacted materials to state and local prosecutors to facilitate ongoing or future criminal investigations

### Statutory Context

- The measure builds upon the original Epstein Files Transparency Act signed into law in November 2025

- Lawmakers contend the Department of Justice failed to comply with the 2025 statute by withholding over three million documents and applying overly broad redactions

# Questions

What specific legal remedies or penalties does the Epstein Files Transparency Act II impose on the Department of Justice if a court finds records were unlawfully withheld?

How does the timeline for House floor consideration operate once a discharge petition successfully attains 218 signatures under House rules?

What specific privacy protections or redaction standards remain intact under the proposed legislation for non-party individuals mentioned in the files?

How does the bill address potential executive privilege claims by the Department of Justice or the Executive Branch regarding internal decision-making records?

What are the structural differences between the enforcement mechanisms of the November 2025 law and the updated 2026 legislation?

## Answers

### Legal Remedies & DOJ Penalties

- The legislation creates an expedited private right of action permitting state attorneys general, district attorneys, members of Congress, and Epstein survivors to sue the US Attorney General and DOJ in federal court.

- Courts are empowered to issue declaratory judgments, grant injunctions against continued withholding or improper redactions, and issue writs of mandamus or equitable relief to compel full compliance.

- Plaintiffs who prevail can also be awarded attorney's fees and litigation costs.

- Additionally, the bill establishes federal criminal penalties for government officials who knowingly conceal, destroy, or unlawfully withhold mandated Epstein-related records.

### House Floor Discharge Timeline

- Once a discharge petition reaches the required 218 signatures, the motion is placed on the Discharge Calendar, where it must remain for a mandatory waiting period of seven legislative days.

- Following the seven-day period, the motion becomes privileged to be called up for consideration on the floor exclusively on the second or fourth Monday of a month.

- Debate on the discharge motion is limited to 20 minutes (split equally between proponents and opponents); if the motion passes, the House proceeds directly to floor consideration of the underlying legislation.

### Privacy Protections & Redactions

- Under the legislation, strict redactions remain mandatory for personally identifiable information (PII) of victims, minor victims, non-public third-party individuals, and confidential law enforcement sources who are not accused of misconduct.

- However, the bill narrows DOJ redaction discretion by requiring individualized written justifications for every specific redaction rather than broad categorical withholdings, while simultaneously granting survivors private, unredacted access to their own interview and investigative records.

### Executive Privilege Claims

- The bill explicitly prohibits the Department of Justice and Executive Branch from invoking common law privileges, such as the deliberative process privilege, to avoid statutory disclosure obligations.

- By statutorily overriding executive decision-making exemptions, the legislation restricts agency discretion and mandates public disclosure of internal records and agency communications regarding the handling of Epstein files.

### Structural Enforcement Differences

- The original November 2025 statute relied entirely on executive self-compliance and statutory mandates without establishing a direct civil judicial mechanism to enforce compliance.

- The updated 2026 legislation shifts to an active, external enforcement framework by granting standing to third-party stakeholders (state officials, lawmakers, and survivors) to sue the DOJ in federal court, attaching criminal penalties for intentional non-compliance, and prohibiting common law privilege defenses.
