# Background, Methodology and SAR Review

In 2022, investigative staff for Senator Ron Wyden at the Senate Finance Committee began examining financial transactions that supported Jeffrey Epstein’s sex trafficking operations. The investigation focused on large payments made to Epstein by ultra‑wealthy individuals and the compliance failures at major Wall Street banks that enabled Epstein’s criminal activity.

The inquiry was prompted by a report commissioned by the Apollo Global Management Board of Directors. The report revealed that billionaire Leon Black paid Epstein \$158 million over five years for purported tax and estate planning advice. This amount far exceeded typical compensation for comparable tax advisory services. As a result, Senate Finance Committee staff conducted a detailed review of Epstein’s compensation scheme, the tax strategies he allegedly devised for Black, and other irregularities involving Black’s financial dealings with Epstein.

## In‑Camera Review of Suspicious Activity Reports

As part of the investigation, bipartisan staff from the Senate Finance Committee conducted an in‑camera review of Suspicious Activity Reports (SARs) filed with the U.S. Treasury Department. The review took place on February 14, 2024, at Treasury headquarters.

These SARs contained extensive evidence of major compliance failures by several Wall Street banks, including:

- JPMorgan Chase  

- Deutsche Bank  

- Bank of America  

Although other banks appeared in the SARs, these three institutions became the primary focus of the investigation.

## Epstein’s Banking History

- Epstein banked at JPMorgan Chase from 1998 to 2013.  

- After JPMC forced him out in 2013, Epstein transferred his assets to Deutsche Bank, where he remained a major client until his death in 2019.  

- Bank of America held accounts for Leon Black that were used to send Epstein approximately \$170 million between 2012 and 2017.

## Evidence of Widespread AML Failures

The SARs revealed that several major banks likely violated federal anti‑money‑laundering laws by failing to contemporaneously report Epstein’s suspicious financial activity. These failures were not isolated mistakes. Instead, banks repeatedly chose not to report thousands of suspicious transactions.

The SARs showed that banks waited years — in some cases more than a decade — before flagging Epstein’s suspicious activity to the Treasury Department. This delay violated federal law requiring timely reporting.

## Retroactive SAR Filings

Banks filed thousands of SARs retroactively only after Epstein’s 2019 arrest. Examples include:

- On September 26, 2019, JPMC flagged 4,725 wire transfers totaling nearly \$1.1 billion flowing in and out of Epstein’s accounts.  

- On August 13, 2019, JPMC flagged 469 additional wire transfers totaling \$201 million, including payments to women in Russia, Belarus and Turkmenistan using correspondent accounts at now‑sanctioned Russian banks.  

- In 2019, Deutsche Bank retroactively flagged 1,140 wire transfers totaling \$147 million.  

- In 2020, Bank of America retroactively flagged \$170 million in payments from Leon Black to Epstein.

These retroactive filings raise serious questions about why banks waited so long to identify and report Epstein’s suspicious financial activity. JPMC knew enough to terminate Epstein as a client in 2013, yet did not report most of his suspicious transactions until 2019.

## Nature of Suspicious Transactions

The retroactively flagged transactions included:

- thousands of wire transfers  

- major cash withdrawals  

- payments to women and girls  

- correspondent banking activity in high‑risk foreign jurisdictions  

- tens of millions in payments to Ghislaine Maxwell  

In total, more than \$1.4 billion moved through Epstein’s accounts over nearly two decades.

## Additional Records Reviewed

Following the SAR review, investigators examined bank records unsealed during lawsuits filed by Epstein’s victims, the U.S. Virgin Islands government, and news organizations. After passage of the Epstein Files Transparency Act (EFTA), investigators also reviewed documents unsealed by the Department of Justice.

These records included:

- internal bank correspondence  

- SARs related to Epstein’s accounts  

- due diligence documents  

- estate planning structures  

- business records  

Taken together, these documents revealed how Wall Street banks made decisions regarding Epstein’s accounts and how senior executives protected Epstein from scrutiny.

## Why Banks Protected Epstein

Investigators identified two primary reasons banks shielded Epstein:

### 1. His Money  

Epstein was an ultra‑wealthy client who generated millions in fees for JPMC every year. Within the bank, he was part of an elite group known as the “wall of cash.”

### 2. His Friends’ Money  

Bankers believed Epstein had influence over other wealthy individuals, including Leon Black. Even after terminating Epstein as a client, JPMC executives continued working with him informally because he could refer lucrative clients.

## Purpose of This Report

This report outlines:

- findings from Senator Wyden’s investigation  

- proposed reforms to federal anti‑money‑laundering laws  

- recommendations for federal regulators and the Department of Justice  

The report calls for prompt action to hold banks and their employees accountable for compliance failures that enabled Epstein’s sex trafficking operations for nearly two decades.
