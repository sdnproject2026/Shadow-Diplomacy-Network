## Europeans investigating Epstein 

Some European law enforcement agencies chasing leads spurred by information in the Epstein files say the Justice Department has not responded to their requests.

September 9, 2026 at 6:00 a.m. 

By Maegan Vazquez

BERLIN — European law enforcement agencies chasing leads from the U.S. investigative files on deceased convicted sex offender Jeffrey Epstein say that one year after the first batch of files was released, the Justice Department has yet to respond to their requests for help.


In the United States, no charges have resulted from the release of the Epstein files. But the documents exposed the financier’s relationships with influential people around the world — both before and after he pleaded guilty in 2008 to prostitution charges — and have sparked a slew of international investigations.


Several European law enforcement officials said in interviews that DOJ has not responded to their requests for mutual legal assistance, which could permit foreign investigators to obtain evidence, information and testimony from the U.S.


Epstein was arrested on federal sex trafficking charges in 2019 and died in federal custody later that year in what officials ruled a suicide. Judges and lawmakers say that over decades, he abused, trafficked and molested women and girls, many of whom have come forward in court and in other public forums.


The Justice Department said it doesn’t comment on the status of mutual legal assistance requests with foreign law enforcement partners. A DOJ spokesperson told The Telegraph: “The department of justice has not refused to assist any jurisdiction investigating potential criminal conduct related to Jeffrey Epstein.”


Poland says it is continuing to pursue an investigation related to the Epstein files. Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk said in February that his country would open an investigation that included a focus on possible links between Epstein and Russian intelligence.


Przemysław Nowak, a spokesman for the National Prosecutor’s Office of Poland, said his office has sought “the disclosure of unredacted documents from the so-called Epstein Files relating to any Polish aspects of the case.”


The Justice Department has not responded to Warsaw’s request in April for mutual legal assistance, Nowak said.


In Norway, three prominent figures face corruption charges related to their relationship with Epstein: former Norwegian diplomats Terje Rød-Larsen and Mona Juul, and former Norwegian prime minister Thorbjørn Jagland. All deny wrongdoing.


Rød-Larsen’s lawyer, John Christian Elden, welcomed efforts to obtain additional evidence from DOJ and said prosecutors requested investigative steps at the International Peace Institute in New York.


Marianne Bender, a state prosecutor for Økokrim, said the U.S. has not fulfilled Norway’s request for information, calling the delay “very unfortunate.”


Latvia’s State Police say they have identified one Latvian victim and have sent several requests for mutual legal assistance to U.S. authorities, with no responses received.


Britain submitted a formal request for unredacted Epstein files after informal discussions with DOJ failed. Police investigated Lord Peter Mandelson in February for alleged misconduct related to Epstein. DOJ and FBI have not responded to the formal request.


Last September, a House committee began releasing Epstein-related files received from the Trump administration. Lawmakers complained the information was incomplete. Congress later voted overwhelmingly to force DOJ to release all Epstein material.


American lawmakers continue pressing DOJ for transparency, saying relevant documents are still missing and some released material is unnecessarily redacted.


The DOJ Office of International Affairs has long faced operational challenges, including high turnover, outdated technology and heavy caseloads, according to a 2021 Inspector General report.


Samer Korkor, a former DOJ attorney, said mutual legal assistance requests are inherently complex, especially when records are voluminous or redactions contested.


Germany’s federal police remain in contact with U.S. authorities regarding possible crimes by Germans suggested by the Epstein files. Green Party lawmakers Konstantin von Notz and Irene Mihalic have urged an investigation, but the government says it has no information indicating criminal links.


Federal criminal police have asked the FBI to involve German authorities in any cases with relevant connections.

## EU Options

### Compel US DOJ Cooperation

European governments have several theoretical pathways  -  legal, diplomatic, treaty‑based, and litigation‑based  -  that could be used to pressure or compel the US

Department of Justice to cooperate with Epstein‑related investigations and release unredacted documents. 

The following is speculative but grounded in real mechanisms.

## 1. Treaty‑Based Obligations

### Mutual Legal Assistance Treaties

MLATs bind the US to provide evidence, records, and testimony to foreign criminal investigations.

Nearly every EU member state has a bilateral MLAT with the US

MLAT leverage includes:

- Formal diplomatic protest for treaty non‑compliance

- State Department escalation

- Suspension of reciprocal cooperation

- Treaty‑violation proceedings

### US–EU Mutual Legal Assistance Agreement 

(2003)

A multilateral treaty binding the US to all EU states simultaneously.

Covers:

- Human trafficking

- Sexual exploitation of minors

- Organized crime

- Cross‑border criminal investigations

Epstein‑related crimes fall squarely within its scope.

EU states could argue the US is violating a multilateral obligation.

### Council of Europe Budapest Convention 

(Cybercrime)

The US is a signatory.

Obligates:

- Rapid evidence sharing

- Preservation of digital records

- Cross‑border investigative cooperation

If Epstein files contain digital communications, this treaty applies.

### UN Convention Against Transnational Organized Crime 

(UNTOC)

Both the US and EU states are parties.

Sex trafficking is explicitly covered.

Allows:

- Formal complaints

- UN review mechanisms

- Pressure through UNODC

## 2. Diplomatic Pressure Tools

### Coordinated EU Demarche

A unified diplomatic protest accusing DOJ of:

- Non‑compliance

- Treaty breach

- Obstruction of international investigations

### European Commission Intervention

The Commission can:

- Issue formal requests to the US

- Tie DOJ cooperation to broader US–EU negotiations

- Launch a justice‑cooperation review

### European Court of Human Rights 

(ECHR)

Victims in Europe could argue:

- DOJ withholding evidence prevents justice

- Violates Article 6 (fair trial) or Article 3 (protection from abuse)

ECHR rulings can force EU states to escalate pressure on the US

### Europol Joint Investigation Team 

(JIT)

A JIT can formally request US cooperation.

If DOJ refuses:

- Europol can issue a non‑cooperation finding

- EU states can escalate diplomatically

- US risks losing access to certain Europol intelligence streams

## 3. Hard‑Pressure Options

### Suspension of MLAT Reciprocity

EU states could threaten:

“If DOJ refuses to honor MLAT obligations, we will suspend cooperation with US requests.”

This is extremely serious and rarely used.

### EU Parliament Resolution

Parliament can:

- Declare DOJ non‑compliant

- Demand release of unredacted files

- Pressure Commission and Council

- Tie cooperation to trade or security negotiations

### Targeted Sanctions

If DOJ is seen as obstructing investigations involving:

- Human trafficking

- Abuse of minors

- Corruption

EU states could invoke:

- Magnitsky‑style sanctions

- Human‑rights sanctions

## 4. Litigation‑Based Pressure

### Foreign Government Petitions in US Federal Court

EU governments can petition US courts to compel DOJ compliance under:

- MLAT enforcement

- Administrative Procedure Act

- Evidence‑access motions

- Victims’ rights statutes

### Victim‑Driven Litigation

European victims could file:

- Civil suits

- Discovery motions

- FOIA lawsuits

- Crime‑victim rights petitions

Courts can order DOJ to produce documents, bypassing DOJ entirely.
