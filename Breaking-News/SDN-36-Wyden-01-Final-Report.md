# Executive Summary and Key Findings

Senator Wyden’s investigative staff on the Senate Committee on Finance conducted a multi‑year investigation into financial transactions that enabled Jeffrey Epstein’s sex trafficking operations. The investigation found that major compliance failures at several Wall Street banks allowed Epstein to move money freely for years without proper scrutiny.

Banks repeatedly failed to report — or chose not to report — suspicious financial activity to federal law enforcement. This allowed Epstein to send cash payments and wire transfers to victims, associates, and collaborators around the world. His crimes were hiding in plain sight because bankers who should have asked questions did not.

The investigation determined that JPMorgan Chase (JPMC), Deutsche Bank, and Bank of America likely violated federal anti‑money‑laundering laws by failing to screen and report Epstein’s suspicious financial transactions in a timely manner.

## Awareness at Senior Levels

A review of Treasury Department records and internal bank documents shows that top executives at major Wall Street banks were aware of Epstein’s suspicious financial activity for years. These executives withheld information from the U.S. government and protected Epstein from scrutiny.

Wall Street banks looked the other way as Epstein:

- withdrew millions of dollars in cash with no clear business purpose  

- executed thousands of suspicious wire transfers worth more than \$1 billion  

- received more than \$170 million from billionaire Leon Black without proper due diligence  

- moved money through high‑risk foreign jurisdictions  

Documents show that JPMC’s senior leadership repeatedly protected Epstein as a client despite warnings from internal compliance personnel. Even after formally terminating Epstein in 2013 due to human trafficking concerns, JPMC waited years to report his activity to regulators. Senior bankers continued working with Epstein informally, using him as a source of referrals for other ultra‑wealthy clients.

## Required Federal Action

The Department of Justice, Treasury Department, Federal Reserve, and Office of the Comptroller of the Currency must conduct thorough investigations into the activities described in this report. These investigations should include the conduct of individual bankers involved in handling Epstein‑related transactions.

The report also proposes legislative reforms to federal anti‑money‑laundering laws to deter future compliance failures involving ultra‑wealthy clients. Banks cannot be allowed to ignore federal law simply because they fear alienating wealthy customers.

## Key Findings

- Bank of America likely violated federal anti‑money‑laundering laws by failing to properly screen and report \$170 million in payments from Leon Black to Jeffrey Epstein.

- Leon Black was Epstein’s single largest source of funding. Epstein relied heavily on Black’s payments to bankroll his sex trafficking activity.

- JPMC likely violated federal anti‑money‑laundering laws by underreporting Epstein’s suspicious financial activity to the U.S. Treasury Department.

- Multiple top executives at JPMC were involved in major compliance failures related to Epstein. Several bankers who personally handled Epstein’s accounts and failed to report suspicious transactions still hold senior positions at the bank.

- After forcing Epstein to leave the bank, JPMC executives withheld information from the U.S. government and continued working with Epstein as a referral source for other ultra‑wealthy clients, including Leon Black.

- JPMC executives coached Epstein on how to withdraw cash through shell companies instead of personal accounts, helping him conceal information from compliance personnel and regulators.

- Senior JPMC executives had knowledge of the pervasive presence of young women or underage girls at Epstein’s properties.

- Deutsche Bank ignored massive cash withdrawals for questionable purposes, protecting its lucrative business relationship with Epstein.

- JPMC, Bank of America, and Deutsche Bank failed to request business records to substantiate or verify the purpose of major suspicious transactions involving Epstein.
