# Government Perjures

If the government knowingly presents perjured testimony and the remaining evidence proves innocence, the legal system provides specific "collateral attacks" to dismantle the conviction. 

These moves are designed to challenge the integrity of the original trial process itself.

## Mooney v. Holohan

- 1935

- The Supreme Court ruled that a conviction is a nullity if the state contrived it through a pretense of trial by using perjured testimony.

- This established that "deliberate deception" by the government violates the Fourteenth Amendment.

## Alcorta v. Texas

- 1957

- In this case, a witness gave false testimony that created a false impression of the defendant's relationship with the victim.

- The prosecutor knew the testimony was false but did not correct it. The Supreme Court overturned the conviction because the lie was material to the sentence the defendant received.

## Napue v. Illinois

- 1959

- The Court held that the failure of the prosecutor to correct testimony which he knew to be false denied the defendant due process.

- This applies even if the government did not solicit the lie, but simply allowed it to go uncorrected.

## Best Next Moves 

### 1. File a Petition for a Writ of Habeas Corpus

- This is the primary tool for a defendant to challenge the lawfulness of their imprisonment.

- Under 28 U.S.C.

- § 2254 (for state cases) or § 2255 (for federal cases), the defendant argues that the conviction was obtained in violation of the U.S. Constitution because of the government's knowing use of perjury.

### 2. Request a Mandatory Disclosure Hearing

- The defense should move for a hearing to compel the government to reveal all "impeachment" material under the Giglio standard.

- This forces the prosecution to turn over internal files that might prove they were aware the witness was lying, which can be used to permanently disqualify the testimony.

### 3. Motion for a New Trial Based on Factual Innocence

- Unlike a standard appeal, which looks for procedural errors, this move presents the new proof that the government lied and that the defendant is factually innocent.

- If the perjury was the "lynchpin" of the government's case, proving its falsity can lead a judge to vacate the verdict immediately.

### 4. File for a Writ of Error Coram Nobis

- This rare "extraordinary remedy" is used when a person is no longer in custody (e.g., they finished their sentence) but still suffers legal consequences from a conviction based on government fraud.

- It asks the court to "reach back" and vacate the conviction because of a fundamental error that was not known at the time of the trial.

### 5. Demand a Dismissal "With Prejudice"

- If the court agrees that the government committed "outrageous misconduct" by lying, the defense should ask the judge to dismiss the case "with prejudice."

- This is the ultimate victory, as it forbids the government from ever charging the defendant for that specific crime again, regardless of what other evidence they might find later.

## Pros and Cons

### Habeas Corpus Petitions

Pro

- It allows the court to consider evidence that was never seen by the original jury, such as proof of the government's secret deals with the lying witness.

Con

- The legal "standard of review" is very high; the defendant must often prove that the error had a "substantial and injurious effect" on the verdict.

### Dismissal With Prejudice

Pro

- Provides absolute finality and prevents a "war of attrition" where the government tries to re-prosecute the case without the lying witness.

Con

- Judges are extremely reluctant to grant this unless the prosecutor's behavior was intentionally malicious, as it takes the decision out of the hands of a future jury.

### Actual Innocence Claims

Pro

- If successful, it completely clears the defendant's name and often qualifies them for state-mandated compensation for wrongful imprisonment.

Con

- Proving "actual innocence" is legally much harder than proving "reasonable doubt," as the burden of proof shifts largely to the defendant.

## Impact of Perjury

### Criminal Conviction - Result

- Vacated.

- If the government’s knowing perjury is the only reason for the conviction, the court will set aside the judgment and order a release or a new trial.

### Civil Judgment - Result

- Vacated and Sanctioned.

- In civil cases, the "fraud on the court" doctrine allows a judge to dismiss the entire case and potentially order the government to pay punitive damages to the victim.

### Acquittal - Result

- Finality.

- Even if the government later proves the defendant lied to get an acquittal, Double Jeopardy prevents a retrial. However, the defendant can be charged with a new crime

- Perjury.

# Post-conviction Motions

## Perjury

If a legal decision is based on perjury that is later proven beyond a reasonable doubt, the decision is not automatically deleted, but it becomes highly vulnerable to being vacated. The legal system uses specific post-conviction motions to address "tainted" evidence.

## Significant Cases 

### United States v. Agurs

- 1976

- This case refined the standards for when a trial is considered "unfair" due to the presence of false information or withheld evidence.

- It clarified that a conviction must be set aside if there is any reasonable likelihood that the false testimony could have affected the judgment of the jury.

## Pros and Cons

### Retrial

Pro

- Restores the defendant's right to a fair trial while still allowing the state to seek justice using legitimate evidence.

Con

- Can be difficult for victims and witnesses who must testify again years later, and evidence may have degraded over time.

### Vacating the Judgment

Pro

- Immediately halts the enforcement of a wrongful sentence or a fraudulent financial penalty.

Con

- Does not technically "acquit" the person; it simply returns the case to its starting position, leaving the accused in legal limbo until the prosecutor decides whether to refile charges.

### Double Jeopardy Protections

Pro

- Protects individuals from being harassed by the government with endless trials if they were acquitted, even if the acquittal involved shady testimony.

Con

- Can lead to a perceived "miscarriage of justice" where a person who is later proven to have lied to get off "not guilty" cannot be tried again for the original crime.

## Impact of Perjury

### Criminal Conviction

- Typically results in a retrial because the "guilty" verdict is considered tainted, and the defendant has a right to be tried based on truth.

### Civil Judgment

- The judgment is usually vacated.

- This prevents a plaintiff or defendant from profiting from a lie told during a lawsuit.

### Acquittal

- The case remains closed.

- Because of Double Jeopardy, the government generally cannot retry a person for the same crime even if they admit they perjured themselves to win.

# Government False Testimony

When the federal or state government knowingly presents false testimony, the legal system views it as a fundamental breakdown of "Ordered Liberty." 

Under the Due Process Clause, the government has a unique duty not just to win cases, but to ensure that justice is served through truthful evidence.

## Pyle v. Kansas

- 1942

- This case expanded the "Mooney" doctrine by clarifying that the knowing use of perjured testimony, combined with the deliberate suppression of evidence favorable to the accused, constitutes a constitutional violation.

- The Court held that even if a trial record appears "regular on its face," the underlying use of coerced or false testimony by state authorities entitles a petitioner to seek release through habeas corpus.

## United States v. Giglio

- 1972

- In a federal case involving a forged money order, the Court ruled that the government is a single entity.

- It held that if one federal prosecutor makes a deal with a witness (such as a promise of leniency), the trial prosecutor is responsible for disclosing that deal - even if they were personally unaware of it.

- Because the witness lied about the deal on the stand and the government failed to correct it, the conviction was overturned.

## Pros and Cons

### Retrial

Pro

- Retrials allow the legal system to "cure" the constitutional error by removing the perjured evidence while still holding the defendant accountable if legitimate evidence exists.

Con

- The passage of time between the original trial and a retrial often leads to faded witness memories or lost physical evidence, making a fair second trial difficult to achieve.

### Vacating the Judgment

Pro

- This provides an immediate remedy for a person wrongfully imprisoned by stopping the enforcement of a tainted sentence and restoring their legal status to "pretrial."

Con

- It does not result in an automatic acquittal; the individual may remain in custody or under indictment for an extended period while the government decides whether to pursue the case again.

### Double Jeopardy Protections

Pro

- These protections prevent the government from using its vast resources to repeatedly try an individual for the same crime until it eventually secures a conviction.

Con

- In cases where a defendant is acquitted based on their own perjury (or that of their witnesses), Double Jeopardy typically prevents the state from ever retrying them for that specific crime, even if the lie is later confessed.

## Impact of Perjury

### Criminal Conviction

- A conviction is usually vacated and a retrial ordered if the perjury is material.

- This is because the "integrity of the judicial process" is considered more important than the finality of the original verdict.

### Civil Judgment

- The court will typically vacate the judgment and may impose heavy sanctions on the lying party.

- Civil courts have broad equitable powers to "undo" any financial gain obtained through fraud on the court.

### Acquittal

- The decision generally remains final.

- While the individual could potentially be charged with the separate crime of perjury, the "Double Jeopardy" clause of the Fifth Amendment prevents a second trial for the original offense.

# Brady Lists

A "Brady List" (sometimes called a "Giglio List" or "No-Call List") is a database maintained by a prosecutor's office containing the names of law enforcement officers who have a documented history of misconduct that could compromise their credibility as witnesses in court.

The name comes from the 1963 Supreme Court case Brady v. Maryland, which established that prosecutors have a constitutional duty to disclose any evidence favorable to the defense (exculpatory evidence). 

This was later expanded by Giglio v. United States (1972) to include information that could be used to impeach the credibility of a government witness, such as a police officer.

## Identification of Misconduct

Prosecutors and police departments track specific behaviors that trigger a "Brady" designation. This typically includes sustained findings of:

### Lying or Perjury

- Making false statements in reports, during internal investigations, or under oath.

### Evidence Tampering

- Fabricating, planting, or altering physical evidence.

### Bias

- Documented instances of racial, religious, or other discriminatory prejudice.

### Criminal Acts

- Past or pending criminal charges against the officer.

## The Disclosure Process

When an officer on the list is involved in a criminal case (as the arresting officer or a witness), the prosecutor must notify the defense attorney.

The defense can then use this information to impeach the officer during cross-examination, arguing to the jury that the officer's testimony should not be trusted because of their history of dishonesty.

## Employment Consequences

- Being "Brady-listed" is often a "career-ending" event for an officer.

- Since their testimony is easily discredited, prosecutors may refuse to file any new cases where that officer is the primary witness.

- Because an officer who cannot testify is unable to perform an essential job function, many departments will terminate the officer or move them to permanent administrative "desk duty."

## Pros and Cons 

### Accountability

Pro

- It creates a permanent record of dishonesty, preventing "problem officers" from hiding their past misconduct when moving between different police departments.

Con

- There are no national standards for what conduct lands an officer on a list; a "lie" could range from a minor reporting error to major perjury, but both can have the same career impact.

### Constitutional Fairness

Pro

- It protects a defendant’s right to a fair trial by ensuring the jury knows if the person testifying against them has a history of fabrication.

Con

- Many lists are not public, and some prosecutors fail to maintain them diligently, meaning "tainted" testimony still enters the courtroom.

### Administrative/Due Process

Pro

- It forces police departments to maintain higher internal standards of truthfulness (often called "No-Lies" policies).

Con

- Officers often claim the lists are "weaponized" by management or prosecutors to get rid of whistleblowers or political rivals without the usual "red tape" of union-protected disciplinary hearings.
