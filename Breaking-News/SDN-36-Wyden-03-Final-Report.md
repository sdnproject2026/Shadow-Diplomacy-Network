# Federal Law Requires Timely Reporting of Suspicious Financial Transactions

Under the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA), financial institutions must maintain anti‑money‑laundering (AML) programs that conduct ongoing due diligence and promptly report suspicious transactions to the U.S. Treasury Department. Banks are legally required to monitor customer activity and flag suspicious transactions in real time to prevent the financial system from being used for criminal activity, including human trafficking.

AML programs must include:

- trained compliance departments  

- transaction monitoring software  

- know‑your‑customer (KYC) procedures  

- customer due diligence (CDD)  

- escalation processes for unusual or suspicious activity  

These systems are intended to identify patterns such as:

- large movements of cash  

- unusual wire transfers  

- payments lacking a clear business purpose  

## SAR Filing Requirements

Under the BSA, banks must file Suspicious Activity Reports (SARs) when they detect a known or suspected violation of federal law or any transaction that may involve money laundering.

A SAR must be filed if a bank knows, suspects, or has reason to suspect that a transaction:

- has no business or apparent lawful purpose  

- is not the type of transaction the customer would normally conduct  

- lacks a reasonable explanation after reviewing available facts  

Banks must file SARs:

- within 30 days of detecting suspicious activity  

- no later than 60 days under any circumstances  

The BSA’s customer due diligence rule also requires banks to:

- understand the nature and purpose of customer relationships  

- conduct ongoing monitoring  

- identify and report suspicious transactions  

## Purpose of AML Laws

AML laws exist to give federal law enforcement real‑time information needed to detect criminal activity and protect public safety. Timely reporting is especially critical when suspicious financial activity involves:

- human trafficking  

- fentanyl and other dangerous narcotics  

- organized crime  

- terrorism financing  

Failing to file timely SARs is both illegal and dangerous. It deprives law enforcement of information needed to stop dangerous criminals.

## JPMC’s Failure to File Timely SARs

JPMorgan Chase terminated Jeffrey Epstein as a client in 2013. However, the bank waited until 2019 to file thousands of SARs on his transactions. Because these SARs were filed years after the suspicious activity occurred, they were not filed within the legally required 30‑day window.

Senator Wyden’s investigators concluded that JPMC failed to comply with the Bank Secrecy Act for the entire duration of its relationship with Epstein.
