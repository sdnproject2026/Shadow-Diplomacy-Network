# Phang vs. Blanche

Judge Emmet Sullivan’s Order

Epstein Files Transparency Act

Section-by-Section Analysis and Document Review

## Overview

On June 25, 2026, U.S. District Judge Emmet G. Sullivan issued a memorandum opinion in _Phang v. Blanche_, granting Katie Phang’s request for a preliminary injunction against Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche.

The lawsuit challenged the Department of Justice’s handling of records covered by the Epstein Files Transparency Act.

Phang alleged that DOJ failed to comply with the statute by withholding records, applying improper redactions, and failing to provide adequate explanations for those redactions.

Judge Sullivan concluded that Phang was likely to succeed on her claim that DOJ had not complied with the statutory disclosure requirements.

### The court ordered DOJ

- release less-redacted versions of specified records; or

- provide legally sufficient explanations showing why the information could lawfully remain withheld.

The deadline imposed by the court was July 2, 2026.

## I. Background of the Litigation

### Plaintiff

Katie Phang filed suit against Todd Blanche in his official capacity as Acting Attorney General.

Phang argued that the Epstein Files Transparency Act created mandatory disclosure obligations and that DOJ could not avoid those requirements by relying solely on ordinary FOIA procedures.

### Defendant

The defendant was:

- Todd Blanche, Acting Attorney General of the United States.

DOJ argued that:

- Phang lacked standing;

- FOIA provided the appropriate mechanism for access requests;

- statutory exemptions justified continued withholding;

- victim privacy protections required extensive redactions.

## II. Judge Sullivan’s Statutory Analysis

### Epstein Files Transparency Act

Requirements

The court analyzed the Act as requiring DOJ to make covered records publicly available, subject to limited exceptions.

### The opinion emphasized:

- Congress imposed a specific disclosure obligation;

- DOJ was required to identify and justify withholding decisions;

- generalized claims of privacy or administrative difficulty were insufficient without document-specific explanations.

## III. Standing and Jurisdiction

### DOJ Argument

DOJ argued that Phang was not entitled to judicial enforcement of the statute and should instead pursue FOIA remedies.

### Court’s Holding

Judge Sullivan rejected that argument.

The court concluded:

- the statute imposed obligations on DOJ;

- Phang could challenge alleged agency noncompliance;

- FOIA was not an adequate substitute for enforcement of the Transparency Act.

## IV. Preliminary Injunction Analysis

The court evaluated the traditional preliminary injunction factors.

### Likelihood of Success

Judge Sullivan found that Phang was likely to succeed because DOJ had not adequately justified several disputed redactions.

### Irreparable Harm

The court found that delayed disclosure created ongoing harm because the statute required timely transparency.

### Balance of Equities

The court weighed:

Government interests:

- victim privacy;

- law-enforcement concerns;

- administrative burden.

Public interests:

- statutory transparency;

- congressional disclosure mandate.

## V. Categories of Records 

Covered by the Order

### A. Email Exchanges

The court identified multiple email chains where DOJ had redacted sender and/or recipient information.

### The court required DOJ to:

- identify the redacted participants; or

- explain why those identities could legally remain withheld.

Reported examples included emails involving Epstein-related communications and references to alleged criminal activity.

### B. Draft Charging Documents

The court addressed redactions involving names appearing in draft charging materials.

The disputed issue was whether names of alleged co-conspirators or associated individuals could remain withheld.

### The court required DOJ to:

- disclose names where required; or

- provide a sufficient legal justification.

### C. FBI Interview Materials

The court addressed FBI interview records containing allegations provided to investigators.

The dispute involved whether DOJ could withhold portions of interview materials based on generalized concerns rather than document-specific legal grounds.

### D. Redaction Log Requirement

The court required DOJ to provide transparency regarding redactions.

The purpose of the log was to allow review of:

- what was withheld;

- why it was withheld;

- what legal authority supported withholding.

## VI. DOJ Response Deadline

### July 2, 2026

DOJ was required to:

- produce less-redacted records; or

- show cause why redactions should remain.

DOJ instead sought additional time and defended its withholding decisions.

## VII. Post-Deadline Status

### As of July 6, 2026:

- DOJ had not released a major new batch of unredacted documents;

- DOJ continued defending its redactions;

- the compliance dispute remained unresolved;

- additional proceedings were expected.

- disclosure obligations;

## Synopsis

The legal battle over the Epstein Files Transparency Act (EFTA) has narrowed to a specific fight over withheld data blocks.

Following U.S. District Judge Emmet Sullivan’s ruling in _Phang v. Blanche_, the Department of Justice (DOJ) was forced to defend its use of heavy redactions.

By cross-referencing the DOJ's compliance filings with structural metadata from decentralized public archives, independent researchers have isolated several key document clusters.

## 1. The Core Legal Disagreement

Judge Sullivan’s order explicitly targets material the DOJ has fought to keep under seal.

The disputed files are split into three main categories:

### The Explicit Media and Communication Cluster

- A set of eight specific email exchanges where Epstein references a "torture video" and explicit interactions. The DOJ has admitted to redacting the senders and recipients, arguing the material involves individuals who were victims but "without context, can appear disturbing" or who "eventually became complicit."

### Raw Investigative Notes

- The underlying, handwritten, or raw field notes from FBI interviews detailing historical abuse allegations involving high-profile political figures, including Donald Trump.

### The Hidden Operational Network

- A heavily redacted draft indictment from the initial federal investigation and a pivotal 2019 email that explicitly names active co-conspirators.

## 4. Second-Party Forensic Repositories

Because the official government document portals are difficult to query and frequently modified, the broader research community relies on three distinct external repositories to cross-reference and preserve the EFTA corpus:

### Epstein-Data.com

- Operates as a full-text indexed search engine. It applies automated Optical Character Recognition (OCR) over raw DOJ PDF dumps, allowing users to search by specific Bates numbers and trace hidden email metadata trails.

### The Oversight Project

- Focuses on pulling the secondary Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) logs. Instead of looking at Epstein's files directly, they track the communication histories _about_ the files, revealing how the DOJ and congressional committees negotiated what to black out.

### Distributed Denial of Secrets (DDoSec)

- A redundant, decentralized cryptographic archive. They host older, unredacted leaks from civil cases (such as _Giuffre v. Maxwell_), allowing investigators to perform side-by-side text comparisons to see which names the government is currently trying to re-redact in 2026.
