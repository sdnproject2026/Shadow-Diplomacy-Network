Attorney General Todd Blanche is turning up the pressure on the Justice Department after
finding that it still has not complied with key portions of his previous orders in the battle
over the release of the Epstein files.
Phang has been keeping audiences up-to-date with the latest breaking developments of
the case on her podcast, Sidebar with Katie Phang.
In an order issued Thursday, U.S. District Judge Emmet Sullivan scheduled an August 13
status hearing and directed the parties to be prepared to address three specific issues
where the DOJ appears to have fallen short. The order comes just one day after the
litigation took another turn, when the Justice Department missed its deadline to answer her
complaint. Phang sought a court order forcing the government to finally respond, and the
DOJ ultimately requested a retroactive extension. Oddly enough, the DOJ filing was made
by U.S. Attorney Jeanine Pirro.
Today’s order suggests Sullivan remains unconvinced by the DOJ’s explanations.
Most notably, Sullivan said the Justice Department failed to provide the documentation he
explicitly ordered to justify its claim that certain names redacted from the Epstein files
belong to victims.
Instead, the government merely asserted that attorneys for certain individuals had
contacted DOJ requesting they be treated as victims.
That wasn’t enough.
“The Defendant’s assertions do not comply with the Court’s Order,” Sullivan wrote, noting
that he had required documentation supporting those claims and not simply
representations from government lawyers.
He ordered the DOJ to come prepared next week to explain what evidence it will actually
provide.
That issue has been central to the case ever since Sullivan ruled in July that the Epstein
Files Transparency Act sharply limits the government’s ability to withhold material. While the
Act permits redactions for victims, the judge has repeatedly insisted the government must
substantiate those claims rather than rely on conclusory assertions.
Sullivan also renewed his scrutiny of another major dispute: the FBI’s handwritten interview
notes.
Back in June, Sullivan ordered the government either to produce the underlying
handwritten notes that formed the basis of FBI FD-302 interview reports or explain why
they should remain withheld.

In Thursday’s order, he observed that nothing in the Epstein Files Transparency Act creates

The judge ordered DOJ lawyers to explain why the handwritten notes cannot simply be
redacted manually, or by some other method, before being released.
The third issue concerns another statutory requirement the Justice Department has yet to
satisfy.
The Epstein Files Transparency Act requires every redaction to be accompanied by a
written justification that must be published in the Federal Register and submitted to
Congress.
The DOJ has acknowledged it intends to comply, but the parties disagree over whether it
has already missed the deadline to do so.
Sullivan ordered the government to be prepared to explain its timetable for fulfilling that
obligation as well.
The hearing marks yet another setback for a Justice Department that has found itself
increasingly on the defensive this week.
On Wednesday, Phang’s legal team filed a motion accusing Blanche of simply ignoring the
Federal Rules of Civil Procedure after missing his June deadline to answer the complaint.
Phang argued the government is technically in default, noted that DOJ never sought an
extension, and urged Sullivan to require Blanche to answer by August 10 rather than allow
the Department to file a belated motion to dismiss.
Rather than answering the complaint, DOJ responded by filing its own motion asking
Sullivan to retroactively stay all filing deadlines and give the government sixty days after the
preliminary injunction proceedings conclude before it must respond. The Department
argued it has been actively litigating the case, that any missed deadline was inadvertent,
and that allowing additional time would promote judicial efficiency while preserving
potential appellate rights.
