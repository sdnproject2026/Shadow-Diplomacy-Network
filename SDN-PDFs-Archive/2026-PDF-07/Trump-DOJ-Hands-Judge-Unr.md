Trump DOJ Hands Judge Unredacted Epstein Records
After Katie Phang’s Court Win
The Justice Department now says it delivered the records directly to the judge after Katie Phang
successfully challenged the government’s Epstein-file redactions.

Justice Department handed unredacted Epstein records to Judge Sullivan for private review.

Judge Sullivan will decide if DOJ's redactions were justified after examining the documents himself.

In a new filing submitted July 30, Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche’s Justice
Department notified U.S. District Judge Emmet Sullivan that it had complied with his July
25 order requiring the government to produce documents supporting a series of contested
redactions.
According to the filing, DOJ delivered hard copies of the documents by hand to Judge
Sullivan’s chambers at 2:28 p.m. Thursday.
The records are being reviewed in camera, meaning privately by the judge rather than being
placed on the public docket. The step allows Sullivan himself to examine what the
government has concealed and determine whether DOJ’s explanations for the redactions
hold up.
The Justice Department insists they do.
“The submitted documents show that the justifications cited in Defendant’s briefing on this
issue support each redaction,” government attorneys wrote.
The filing addresses 10 separate Epstein-file records identified by the court.

DOJ claims most of the disputed redactions conceal the identities of victims or potential
victims, including individuals represented by attorneys who have asked the department to
treat their clients as victims. Other redactions, the government says, protect personally
identifiable information and the identities of law enforcement personnel and an Assistant
U.S. Attorney.
But the filing also contains at least one notable admission.
DOJ says that after taking a closer look at one of the records, EFTA01187999, officials
discovered they had “inadvertent[ly]” redacted Jeffrey Epstein’s own email address.
That redaction, the department says, “has since been lifted.”
The development comes after Sullivan ordered DOJ to provide the court with the
unredacted material necessary to independently test the government’s claims about what it
was withholding.
Phang’s lawsuit has challenged DOJ’s compliance with the Epstein Files Transparency Act
and its decisions to keep information concealed from the public.
The dispute is particularly significant because the court is no longer being asked simply to
take the Justice Department at its word. Sullivan now has the underlying documents in his

DOJ also used Thursday’s filing to say that some of the material remains covered by
protective orders entered in the federal criminal cases against Epstein and Ghislaine
Maxwell in the Southern District of New York.
Those orders, DOJ noted, prohibit the government from publicly disclosing the identities of
individuals contained in discovery.
Protecting Epstein’s victims has not been the central point of dispute. Rather, Phang’s
litigation has forced DOJ to substantiate whether the information it chose to hide actually
falls within legitimate categories that may be withheld.
Thursday’s filing represents the government’s argument, not Judge Sullivan’s conclusion.
DOJ says its documents prove the redactions were justified. Sullivan will now have the
opportunity to examine the unredacted records himself and determine whether he agrees.
And the government has already acknowledged that at least one redaction should not have
been there in the first place.
The next major development could come when Sullivan rules on whether DOJ’s remaining
redactions survive that scrutiny.
