# Don'T Sex Traffic

## Sexual Assault vs. Sex Trafficking

The legal distinction between sexual assault and sex trafficking is significant regarding both the nature of the crime and the severity of the potential penalties.

### Sexual Assault

- Sexual assault is defined as any type of sexual contact or behavior that occurs without the explicit consent of the recipient.

- Legally, this includes crimes such as forced sexual intercourse, forcible sodomy, child molestation, and attempted rape.

- In a criminal context, the focus is on the lack of consent and the use of physical force or the threat of force.

- Civilly, as seen in cases like Carroll v. Trump, a defendant can be found liable for battery or sexual abuse, resulting in significant monetary damages even if criminal charges are not filed or sustained.

### Sex Trafficking

- Sex trafficking is a more complex and severe charge that involves the use of force, fraud, or coercion to exploit an individual for the purpose of a commercial sex act.

- Under federal law, if the victim is under the age of 18, any person who recruits, entices, harbors, transports, provides, or obtains that minor for a commercial sex act is guilty of sex trafficking, regardless of whether force or coercion was used.

- The legal impact of a sex trafficking allegation is far broader than a single assault charge; it implies an organized effort to transport and exploit victims, often involving multiple jurisdictions.

- Penalties for sex trafficking are substantially higher than those for standard sexual assault, often carrying mandatory minimum sentences and fines reaching up to 1,000,000 dollars.

- If the handwritten notes confirm that an individual was driven or flown across state lines (e.g., from Florida to New York or New Jersey) specifically for the purpose of being "introduced" for a sexual encounter, this triggers federal sex trafficking statutes such as the Mann Act.

### Immunity Regarding Sex Trafficking Allegations

The question of whether a President or former President has immunity for such allegations depends on the timing and nature of the acts.

- Under the 2024 Supreme Court ruling in Trump v. United States, a President has absolute immunity for "official acts" performed within their core constitutional authority and presumptive immunity for other official acts.

- However, the Supreme Court explicitly stated that there is no immunity for "unofficial conduct."

- Acts of sexual assault or involvement in sex trafficking are considered private, unofficial acts. They do not fall within the "outer perimeter" of a President's official duties.

- Because the allegations in the Epstein files describe events occurring in the 1980s and 1990s - decades before Donald Trump assumed the presidency - they are strictly private acts.

- Presidential immunity does not shield an individual from prosecution or civil litigation for conduct that occurred before they took office or for private conduct while in office.

- Therefore, Trump does not have legal immunity against criminal charges or civil lawsuits stemming from allegations of sex trafficking or sexual assault.
