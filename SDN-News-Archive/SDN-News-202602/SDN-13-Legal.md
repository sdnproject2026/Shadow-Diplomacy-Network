## The Maxwell Reorganization

- The most significant component of Kirkland's role involved the New York Daily News.

- The paper was "awash in red ink" and facing liquidation.

- Kirkland's restructuring team was tasked with finding a buyer while navigating intense labor disputes with the newspaper's unions.

- The Maxwell case was one of the first "mega-bankruptcies" that helped establish Kirkland & Ellis as a powerhouse in the corporate restructuring field.

# Sweetheart Deal

## 2007

Kirkland & Ellis partners, including Jay Lefkowitz, represented Jeffrey Epstein in negotiations with the US Attorney’s Office for the Southern District of Florida.

## 2008

### Non-Prosecution Agreement

Kirkland & Ellis partners secured a Non-Prosecution Agreement (NPA) for Jeffrey Epstein that is frequently described as one of the most controversial "sweetheart deals" in legal history.

This representation resulted in the controversial non-prosecution agreement that allowed Epstein to plead guilty to lesser state charges rather than federal sex trafficking charges.

## 2019

The firm's prior representation of Jeffrey Epstein became a central point of scrutiny during William Barr’s tenure as Attorney General.

Barr officially recused himself from the Justice Department’s 2019 review of the 2008 Florida plea deal due to his previous partnership at Kirkland & Ellis, though he did not recuse himself from the 2019 sex trafficking case brought by the SDNY.

## Key Figures

The negotiation was led by a high-powered defense team that utilized professional and personal ties to the Department of Justice.

### Jay Lefkowitz

- A senior Kirkland partner and former high-ranking official in the George W. Bush administration.

- He was the primary architect of the plea deal.

### Ken Starr

- A Kirkland partner (and former Solicitor General) who acted as a "fixer," using his high-level political stature to pressure the DOJ to review the case.

### Alexander Acosta

- Then the US Attorney for the Southern District of Florida.

- Notably, Acosta was a former Kirkland & Ellis associate himself, a fact that later fueled intense scrutiny regarding the "revolving door" between the firm and the DOJ.

## The Negotiation Strategy

The Kirkland team employed a "scorched-earth" strategy to derail the federal indictment, which had been drafted and was ready for filing.

### Pressure on Leadership

- When lower-level prosecutors (like Marie Villafaña) pushed for federal charges, Ken Starr wrote an 8-page letter to Mark Filip - then the Deputy Attorney General and also a former Kirkland partner.

- The letter successfully triggered a top-down review of the case.

### The Secret Hotel Meeting

- Negotiations bypassed typical DOJ channels.

- Jay Lefkowitz met with Alexander Acosta at a hotel in West Palm Beach - roughly 70 miles from the US Attorney’s main office - to finalize the terms of the secret agreement.

### Concealment from Victims

- The most legally contentious aspect of the negotiation was the decision to keep the NPA secret.

- Victims were led to believe that federal prosecution was still a possibility long after the deal had been signed, a move that a federal judge later ruled violated the Crime Victims' Rights Act (CVRA).

## Terms of the Sweetheart Deal

The resulting NPA, signed in late 2007 and executed in 2008, provided Epstein with extraordinary protections.

### Federal Immunity

- The DOJ agreed not to prosecute Epstein for any federal crimes related to the investigation.

### Co-Conspirator Protection

- Immunity was extended to four named co-conspirators (including Ghislaine Maxwell) and any "potential co-conspirators," effectively shutting down the FBI's probe into wider networks.

### State Plea

- Epstein pleaded guilty to only two state-level solicitation charges.

### Work Release

- Despite his 13-month sentence, he was granted "work release," allowing him to spend up to 12 hours a day, 6 days a week at his private office instead of a jail cell.

Records indicate his "connection" is often defined by his firm partnership rather than direct billable hours on those specific defense teams.

During the Maxwell Communication Corporation reorganization, William Barr was serving as the 77th US Attorney General under George H.W. Bush.

While he was not at the firm during the peak of the Maxwell restructuring, he joined Kirkland & Ellis shortly after leaving office in 1993.

### 1994–2000

As a partner at Kirkland & Ellis, Barr focused heavily on telecommunications and administrative law.

During this window, the firm continued to manage the long-tail litigation and asset distribution arising from the Maxwell bankruptcy, which affected the interests of the Maxwell family and their creditors.

William Barr was a senior partner at Kirkland & Ellis when the firm successfully negotiated Jeffrey Epstein's Florida plea deal.

While Jay Lefkowitz was the lead attorney of record for Epstein, Barr’s status as a partner at the time created the conflict of interest that necessitated his later recusal as Attorney General in 2019.

### 2017–2018

Before being nominated as Attorney General for a second time, Barr was a partner at the firm while it represented a variety of high-profile corporate clients.

During this time, the firm's historical work for Epstein remained a matter of internal record, though no new active representation of Epstein or the Maxwells by Barr has been substantiated in public dockets from this specific period.

# Professional Conduct Rules

When a partner from a firm like Kirkland & Ellis transitions into a high-ranking Department of Justice (DOJ) role, they are subject to a multi-layered framework of federal statutes, executive orders, and professional conduct rules.

These barriers are designed to prevent the attorney from using their public office to benefit their former firm or its clients.

## Mandatory Recusals

(The Two-Year Rule)

The primary mechanism for preventing conflicts is the mandatory recusal period.

Under federal ethics regulations (5 C.F.R. § 2635.502), appointees must recuse themselves from "particular matters" involving their former employer or former clients.

### Former Employer Recusal

- Attorneys are barred for two years from participating in any matter where Kirkland & Ellis is a party or represents a party.

### Former Client Recusal

- The same two-year ban applies to any specific client the attorney personally represented while at the firm.

### Scope

- This includes not just active litigation, but also investigations, contract negotiations, and applications for federal grants or licenses.

## Financial Divestiture

"Golden Parachute" Bans

To ensure no ongoing financial incentive remains, federal law (18 USC. § 208) prohibits officials from working on matters that affect their own financial interests.

### Partnership Distributions

- Attorneys must receive their final partnership share or "buy-out" before entering government service.

- If any portion of their compensation is tied to the firm's future profits or success in a specific case, they are permanently disqualified from that matter.

### Golden Parachutes

- Under the Ethics Pledge (standard across most modern administrations), appointees are prohibited from accepting "extraordinary payments" from a former employer that are contingent on the individual taking a government position.

### Ongoing Interests

- If an attorney retains a pension or 401(k) managed by the firm, additional screening is required to ensure they do not oversee cases that could impact the firm's overall solvency.

## The "Matter-Specific" Permanent Bar

Under 18 USC. § 207(a)(1), there is a lifetime ban on "switching sides."

- If an attorney worked "personally and substantially" on a specific case (e.g., defending a corporation in a DOJ investigation) while at Kirkland, they can never work on that same matter from the government side.

- This prevents the attorney from using confidential information gained in private practice to assist the government’s prosecution or oversight of the same individual or entity.

## Internal Screening & "Gatekeepers"

To manage these conflicts day-to-day, high-ranking DOJ officials utilize formal screening arrangements.

### Gatekeeping

- A career ethics official (often within the DOJ’s Departmental Ethics Office) acts as a "gatekeeper."

- They review all incoming briefs, meeting requests, and case files before they reach the official’s desk.

### Filtering

- If a matter involves a Kirkland client or the firm itself, the gatekeeper automatically reroutes the file to a different official (such as a career Deputy) to ensure the recused partner has no input or "behind-the-scenes" influence.

## Professional Ethics Rules 

(ABA & State Bars)

Beyond federal law, DOJ attorneys remain bound by Model Rule 1.11 of the American Bar Association.

### Confidentiality

- They are strictly prohibited from using or revealing "protected information" obtained during their time at Kirkland & Ellis.

### Imputation

- While a single attorney's conflict usually does not disqualify the entire DOJ, it requires that the attorney be effectively "walled off" from the rest of the team working on the matter.

#  Epstein RICO Charges

Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations

## More Than One

A priori, RICO focuses on perpetrators, (more than one), and jurisdictions, (more than one), and criminal acts and offenses, (more than one).

## Unredacted

In the event that one perp dies, the RICO cases live on, co-conspirators, or may multiply as new co-conspirators are "unredacted".

## Unique Legal Landscape

In the context of complex cases like the Jeffrey Epstein investigations, the use of RICO statutes creates a unique legal landscape. 

## Enterprise And Pattern

Because RICO targets the enterprise and a pattern of activity rather than just isolated crimes, the distinction between the "same act" and the "same offense" becomes a weapon for prosecutors to bypass traditional double jeopardy protections.

# Foreign Conviction

If perpetrators are convicted in a foreign jurisdiction (e.g., France or the US Virgin Islands), Florida or New York may still pursue state RICO charges for the same act because they are considered different legal offenses against different sovereigns, jurisdictions.

## Florida - New York

Under Florida Statute § 895.02, a "pattern of racketeering activity" requires at least two "predicate acts" that are interrelated. 

In a high-profile case spanning multiple jurisdictions, Florida could charge the following as state-level offenses, even if they were part of a "same act" prosecuted elsewhere.

### Human Trafficking

 (§ 787.06)

- Transporting or recruiting individuals for labor or sexual servitude. If an individual was moved from a foreign country to an estate in Palm Beach, Florida views this as an offense against Florida sovereignty.

### Sexual Battery

 (§ 794.011)

- Any non-consensual sexual act occurring on Florida soil. Each individual instance is a separate "offense" even if they are part of a larger "act" of ongoing abuse.

### Proceeds of Prostitution

 (§ 796.05)

- Managing or profiting from a prostitution enterprise.

### Lewd or Lascivious Exhibition

 (§ 800.04)

- Crimes specifically involving victims under 16, which carry heavy penalties in Florida and are often used as predicate acts for RICO.

### Tampering with Witnesses

 (§ 914.22)

- If a perpetrator or their associate threatened or bribed a witness located in Florida to keep them from speaking to foreign or federal investigators.

### Money Laundering

 (§ 896.101)

- Conducting financial transactions in Florida (e.g., through a Palm Beach bank account) using funds derived from the illegal enterprise.

## The RICO "Complications" List

In a multi-jurisdictional scenario, several factors prevent the "bar statute" from stopping a Florida prosecution, or any other State's prosecution.

And, similar cases may arise in each State as separate sovereigns, each satisfying their own laws apart from prior convictions.

### The Sovereignty Gap

(§ 768.28)

(The "Claims Bill" Exception)

- A conviction in the US Virgin Islands or France is a conviction by a different sovereign. Florida is not "barred" by § 910.11 because that statute only applies to "two or more [Florida] counties."

### The Dual Sovereignty Gap

(Booth v. State, 436 So. 2d 36 (Fla. 1983))

- In a different branch of Florida law (Criminal), you may also hear about a "Sovereignty Gap" regarding Double Jeopardy.

- Under the Dual Sovereignty Doctrine, the State of Florida and the Federal Government are considered separate "sovereigns."

- This creates a gap in constitutional protection where a person can be acquitted in a Florida state court but then immediately prosecuted by the Federal government for the exact same act. 

- The "sovereignty" of each entity prevents the Fifth Amendment's double jeopardy clause from blocking the second trial, for example. 

### The "Pattern" Exception

(§ 90.404(2))

- RICO is legally defined as a "separate offense" from the underlying crimes. You may be convicted of the act (e.g., one instance of trafficking) and also convicted of the RICO offense (the pattern including that act) without violating double jeopardy.

### The Blockburger Element

(§ 775.021(4))

- Florida RICO requires proving an "Enterprise" (an ongoing formal or informal group). A foreign simple assault or trafficking charge does not require proving an "enterprise," meaning the Florida RICO charge is legally a "different offense" even if it uses the same act as evidence.

### Extraterritorial Jurisdiction

 (§ 910.005)

- Florida may prosecute a person for an act committed outside the state if that conduct was a conspiracy to commit a crime inside Florida.

## This is Proof

In a Florida RICO or complex felony case, a prosecutor cannot simply walk into court, show the judge a foreign conviction, and say, "This is proof he did it."

However, they may use that foreign conviction as a powerful tactical bridge to introduce the underlying evidence. Here is how that "proof" actually functions in a Florida courtroom.

# US State "Goes Second"

If a perpetrator is convicted abroad, Florida prosecutors may look at the same physical acts and re-label them as violations of the Florida RICO Act. Because the "victim" is the State of Florida and the "offense" includes the element of an organized enterprise, the previous foreign conviction does not act as a legal bar.

## 1. "Williams Rule" Evidence

In Florida, Rule of Evidence 90.404(2) (known as the Williams Rule) allows prosecutors to introduce evidence of "other crimes, wrongs, or acts."

### The Goal

- Not to prove the defendant is a "bad person," but to show a pattern, motive, intent, or plan.

### The Use

- A prosecutor may tell the jury about the foreign acts to prove that the Florida acts weren't an "accident" but were part of the same "enterprise" (a key requirement for RICO).

## 2. A "Predicate Act"

For a Florida RICO charge, the state must prove a "pattern" of at least two predicate acts.

### The Problem

- Generally, a predicate act must be a violation of Florida law or a specific list of federal/state crimes. A conviction under French or British law doesn't automatically count as a Florida "predicate act."

### The Workaround

- Prosecutors will recharge the same act as a Florida crime (e.g., Human Trafficking under FL Statute § 787.06). They use the evidence from the foreign case to prove this "new" Florida offense, which then serves as a brick in the RICO wall.

## 3. "Collateral Estoppel" 

(The One-Way Street)

This is a legal doctrine that prevents someone from re-litigating a fact that has already been decided.

### Against the Perp

- In civil RICO cases (lawsuits for money), a criminal conviction often automatically stops the defendant from denying the facts (Collateral Estoppel).

### In Criminal Court

- It is much harder. Because a defendant has a Constitutional right to a jury trial in Florida, they may usually force the prosecutor to prove the facts all over again, even if a foreign court already found them guilty.

## 4. Judicial Notice of Foreign Law

Under Florida Statute § 90.202, a Florida judge may take "judicial notice" of the laws of foreign nations.

### The Effect

- The judge may acknowledge that the foreign law exists and that a conviction occurred. While the jury still has to decide if the defendant is guilty of the Florida charges, the existence of the foreign conviction may be used to impeach the defendant if they take the stand and lie about their past.

### Impeachment (Rule 90.610)

- If a defendant testifies in their own defense and claims they have never committed such acts, the prosecutor may use the foreign conviction to "impeach" their credibility, showing the jury the defendant is lying.

### Intent/Motive

-  The foreign acts prove the perp's "state of mind" for the Florida crimes. 

### Sentencing Points (Statute § 921.0021)

- If the perpetrator is convicted in Florida, the foreign conviction is officially scored as "Prior Record." Under Florida’s sentencing guidelines, a foreign felony is mapped to the "analogous" Florida crime, significantly increasing the mandatory minimum prison time for the new Florida offenses.

### Big Risk

- If the foreign country has significantly lower "due process" standards than the US (e.g., no right to a lawyer, or trials in absentia), a Florida judge might exclude the conviction entirely, fearing it would "poison" the jury and lead to an overturned verdict on appeal.
