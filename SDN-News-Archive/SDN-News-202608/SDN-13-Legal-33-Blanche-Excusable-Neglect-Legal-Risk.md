# AG Todd Blanche

## Transition From Acting AG

### Legal Risk

The central point is that Blanche's change from “acting Attorney General” to confirmed Attorney General does not erase anything that happened while he was acting AG. It changes his institutional position going forward, but the court case concerns conduct by the DOJ and Blanche in his official capacity during the earlier period. 

The confirmation therefore creates a more complicated political and accountability environment, rather than providing a legal reset.

I also need to flag several important corrections to the supplied article. 

The underlying court record confirms the Phang v. Blanche case, the June 25 preliminary injunction, and that Blanche was sued in his capacity as Acting Attorney General. 

The court record does not, from the materials I could verify, establish every allegation in the article about a missing affidavit, a deliberate tactical delay, or sanctions being imposed. 

Moreover, the Supreme Court's _Pioneer_ standard does not categorically exclude _inadvertence_ or _carelessness_ from “excusable neglect”; it expressly says the doctrine can encompass them depending on the circumstances.

## Impact on the Proceedings

### 1. Confirmation does not moot the lawsuit

The case is an APA action against Todd Blanche in his official capacity. Judge Emmet Sullivan's June 25 opinion expressly identifies the defendant as “Todd Blanche, in his official capacity as Acting Attorney General of the United States.”

That matters because the litigation is fundamentally about what the Department of Justice must do, not about whether Blanche personally possesses some special legal status as an acting officer.

Once Blanche became the confirmed Attorney General, the government could seek to update the caption or substitute the proper official designation, but that would ordinarily be a procedural housekeeping issue rather than a reason for the underlying controversy to disappear.

In practical terms:

- Acting AG Blanche → confirmed AG Blanche

does not mean:

- old DOJ obligations → extinguished.

The DOJ remains subject to the court's orders and the statute.

### 2. The confirmation can actually make compliance more consequential

While acting, Blanche could characterize the dispute as occurring during an interim period of leadership.

After confirmation, that argument becomes substantially weaker politically.

The June 25 opinion already found that Phang was likely to succeed on relevant APA claims and granted her preliminary injunction. 

The opinion describes the Epstein Act as requiring unusually rapid disclosure and identifies particular categories of material that Phang alleged had been unlawfully withheld or redacted.

### Continuing Obligation

That creates a continuing obligation for the DOJ leadership.

If subsequent noncompliance occurs after confirmation, congressional investigators can ask a much simpler question:

> Who was actually responsible for enforcing the court's order once Blanche was the confirmed Attorney General?

That does not automatically establish personal liability for Blanche, but it increases the political and institutional significance of subsequent failures.

### 3. The “excusable neglect” issue is narrower than the article suggests

This is one of the most important legal corrections.

Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 6(b)(1)(B), a court may extend a deadline after it expires when the failure resulted from “excusable neglect.”

The Supreme Court's _Pioneer_ Investment Services v. Brunswick Associates decision says the inquiry considers the circumstances surrounding the omission, including:

- prejudice to the opposing party;

- length of the delay and its effect on the proceedings;

- the reason for the delay and whether it was within the party's control; and

- whether the party acted in good faith.

Importantly,_Pioneer_says “excusable neglect” can sometimes include inadvertence, mistake, or carelessness.

So the legal argument is not simply:

> “They missed the deadline, therefore excusable neglect is impossible.”

The much stronger argument for Phang would be:

> “Whatever label DOJ puts on the failure, the actual reason, degree of control, length of delay, impact on the proceedings and evidence of good faith do not justify relief.”

If DOJ's own filings demonstrate that lawyers consciously chose not to file until they believed another stage of the litigation was complete, that could materially weaken the good-faith/reason-for-delay portions of the_Pioneer_analysis.

But that factual proposition needs to be established from the actual filings - not merely inferred from commentary.

# Legal Risk to Blanche and DOJ

There are several different categories of risk, and they should not be conflated.

### A. Procedural risk

The immediate risk is that Judge Sullivan refuses to excuse the late filing and requires DOJ to comply with the underlying order.

That could produce:

- mandatory production;

- supplemental declarations;

- detailed explanations for redactions;

- additional hearings;

- accelerated compliance schedules;

- possible fee or cost consequences in appropriate circumstances; and

- potentially stronger enforcement orders if noncompliance continues.

### B. Credibility risk

This may be more important than the missed deadline itself.

Federal litigation depends heavily on judicial confidence in representations made by government counsel.

If DOJ says:

> “This was an inadvertent oversight.”

but documents show that counsel deliberately made a strategic decision concerning when to respond, the court could view the explanation as incomplete or misleading.

That does not automatically constitute contempt or misconduct. But it can affect how the judge evaluates future representations.

For an agency defending hundreds of thousands or millions of pages of redactions, credibility with the judge is an extremely valuable litigation asset.

### C. Enforcement/contempt risk

A missed filing deadline and contempt are not synonymous.

A court generally has substantially greater enforcement authority when there is a clear order requiring a party to perform a specific act and the party knowingly fails to comply.

Therefore, the legally important progression is:

missed deadline → court finds violation → clear compliance order → continued noncompliance

rather than:

missed deadline → automatic contempt.

If DOJ ultimately complies after an adverse ruling, the contempt question can look very different from a situation in which DOJ repeatedly refuses to comply.

The August 13 hearing is therefore important because it can clarify whether the dispute is merely about procedural lateness or whether the court perceives an ongoing failure to obey its orders.

# The Epstein Files Transparency Act Makes This Different From Ordinary FOIA Litigation

This is another place where the supplied article understates the significance of the statute.

Congress enacted the Epstein Files Transparency Act as Public Law 119-38 on November 19, 2025. The law requires the Attorney General to make covered unclassified DOJ records relating to Epstein publicly available in searchable and downloadable form.

More importantly, Congress expressly prohibited withholding based on:

- embarrassment;

- reputational harm; or

- political sensitivity.

At the same time, Congress expressly permitted withholding or redaction for categories including victim-identifying information, child sexual-abuse material, certain ongoing investigations, graphic death/injury material, and properly classified national-security information.

That creates an unusually important distinction:

DOJ cannot simply invoke traditional government secrecy interests whenever disclosure is politically uncomfortable.

But neither does the statute require disclosure of everything without exception.

The dispute is therefore likely to center increasingly on:

> Does this particular redaction fall within an expressly permitted statutory exception?

rather than simply:

> Does DOJ have a generally applicable FOIA exemption?

The statute also requires written justification for redactions and a report to Congress describing categories released and withheld.

That gives both Judge Sullivan and Congress additional mechanisms for testing DOJ's explanations.

# Why the Acting-to-Confirmed Transition Matters

There are three distinct effects.

### 1. It strengthens Blanche's institutional authority

A confirmed Cabinet officer has substantially greater political legitimacy than someone serving in an acting capacity.

Blanche can now say, institutionally:

> “I am the Senate-confirmed head of the Department.”

That makes it easier for him to defend DOJ policy before Congress and to direct the department internally.

### 2. It eliminates the “temporary leadership” explanation

The Senate confirmed Blanche 50-49 on August 8, after he had served as acting AG.

That means Blanche enters the Epstein litigation with a clear confirmation record and known congressional concerns about his independence.

His confirmation therefore gives him greater authority but also creates a clearer political accountability trail.

### 3. His previous conduct doesn't become “confirmed”

This distinction is critical.

Senate confirmation means:

> The Senate consented to Blanche serving as Attorney General.

It does not mean:

> The Senate approved or legally ratified every decision Blanche made while acting Attorney General.

Nor does confirmation immunize him from judicial review of DOJ actions.

The court can still examine whether the Department complied with the law and its orders.

# Conflict-of-Interest Issues

This is potentially the most sensitive aspect, but it requires precision.

There are at least three different kinds of conflict questions.

### 1. Actual financial conflict

18 U.S.C. § 208 prohibits an executive-branch employee from personally and substantially participating in a particular matter when the employee has a qualifying financial interest, subject to statutory exceptions and waivers.

Nothing in the materials I verified establishes that Blanche has such a financial interest in the Epstein-files litigation.

Therefore, it would be inappropriate to characterize the situation as an established §208 violation.

### 2. Appearance/impartiality problem

This is more plausible as a governance issue, even where there is no prohibited financial interest.

DOJ's ethics framework recognizes circumstances in which a reasonable person could question an employee's impartiality and provides mechanisms for determining whether participation should continue.

There is also a DOJ-specific regulation, 28 C.F.R. §45.2, concerning participation in criminal investigations or prosecutions where an employee has certain personal or political relationships with people or organizations substantially involved in the matter.

But an important distinction is necessary:

§45.2 concerns criminal investigations/prosecutions.

The Phang litigation is an APA civil case concerning DOJ's disclosure obligations. So one cannot automatically take §45.2 and declare Blanche disqualified from the civil litigation.

### 3. Former-client/loyalty concerns

This is politically more significant.

Blanche previously represented Donald Trump as a private lawyer. That history has generated questions about DOJ independence.

DOJ's Inspector General has separately highlighted the need for policies governing DOJ attorneys' participation in matters involving former clients because such participation can cause a reasonable person to question impartiality.

But again, that does not establish that Blanche is legally disqualified from the Phang case.

# The More Serious Conflict Question

The strongest issue is arguably not:

> “Is Blanche legally prohibited from participating?”

It is:

> Can DOJ credibly demonstrate that decisions concerning politically sensitive Epstein records are being made according to the statute rather than according to the interests of politically powerful individuals?

That is particularly important because Congress specifically prohibited withholding material on the basis of political sensitivity.

If a court were to find that redactions or withholding decisions were actually motivated by political considerations, the consequences could be substantially more serious than a late filing.

The court could then be dealing with a potential conflict between:

DOJ's statutory duty to disclose

and

political interests allegedly benefiting from non-disclosure.

That is the issue that would potentially transform this from an ordinary compliance dispute into a major institutional separation-of-powers controversy.

# What DOJ Can Do Now

The strongest litigation strategy is not necessarily to keep fighting exclusively over “excusable neglect.”

DOJ has several alternatives.

### 1. Make the deadline dispute secondary

DOJ can acknowledge the procedural error while arguing that the underlying merits should still be decided on the substance.

That is consistent with Pioneer, which treats the circumstances of the omission as the central inquiry rather than making the label “inadvertent” dispositive.

### 2. Provide a detailed explanation

Rather than simply saying “oversight,” DOJ could explain:

- who was responsible;

- what deadline was understood;

- when the problem was discovered;

- why the filing was not made;

- how quickly DOJ acted once it discovered the problem;

- whether the government gained any strategic advantage; and

- whether Phang suffered prejudice.

Those facts map directly onto the_Pioneer_framework.

### 3. Demonstrate compliance

The most effective way to reduce judicial hostility may be to show that the underlying order is being obeyed.

That means detailed production and redaction explanations rather than continued procedural disputes.

### 4. Separate Blanche personally from institutional DOJ decisions where appropriate

Because the lawsuit is against Blanche in his official capacity, the government should avoid creating the impression that every operational error by DOJ lawyers was personally directed by Blanche unless the evidence actually establishes that.

The court's June 25 opinion itself frames the defendant as Blanche in his official capacity.

### 5. Consider ethics review where a genuine conflict exists

If a particular Epstein-related matter actually implicates a qualifying personal, political, former-client, or financial relationship, DOJ can use its ethics procedures rather than allowing the conflict issue to become a recurring litigation distraction.

A recusal does not mean the government stops defending its position; another authorized DOJ official can assume responsibility for the affected matter.

# Could Blanche Personally Be Sanctioned?

Possible, but the supplied article gets ahead of the evidence.

There is a major difference between:

sanctions against DOJ/the government

and

personal sanctions against Blanche.

The latter generally requires a much stronger factual basis demonstrating that Blanche himself violated an order, acted improperly, or otherwise satisfied the applicable standard for personal sanctions.

If career or political DOJ lawyers missed a deadline without Blanche personally directing the failure, attributing that conduct personally to him would require additional evidence.

The fact that he was Attorney General - acting or confirmed - does not automatically make him personally liable for every litigation mistake by DOJ counsel.

# What the Confirmation Changes in the August 13 Proceeding

The most significant consequence is accountability, not jurisdiction.

The court's authority over the case does not materially disappear because Blanche was confirmed.

Instead, the hearing now occurs against this backdrop:

June 25: Sullivan grants Phang's preliminary injunction.

August 7/8: Blanche moves from acting AG to confirmed AG after a 50-49 Senate vote.

August 13: the court addresses DOJ compliance and the continuing dispute.

That creates a politically awkward situation for Blanche: the same official who was sued as Acting Attorney General is now the Senate-confirmed Attorney General responsible for the Department's continuing compliance.

But legally, the court should still focus on concrete questions:

1. What did the court order?

2. What deadline did it establish?

3. What did DOJ actually do?

4. Why did it fail to do something on time?

5. Was the failure excusable under Pioneer?

6. Is DOJ now complying?

7. Are challenged redactions authorized by the Epstein Act?

8. Is there evidence of bad faith or improper political considerations?

9. What remedy is necessary?

Those questions are considerably more important legally than the title change itself.

## Bottom line

The acting-to-confirmed transition is legally less important than the article implies, but politically more important than it may appear.

It does not wipe away the June 25 injunction, cure a missed deadline, eliminate the APA case, or immunize Blanche from future judicial scrutiny.

What it does is put the dispute squarely under the authority of a Senate-confirmed Attorney General. If DOJ continues to have compliance problems after confirmation, Blanche has less institutional room to characterize them as artifacts of an interim administration.

The greatest legal danger is therefore not simply the missed deadline. It is a potential combination of:

failure to comply with a court order + weak explanation for the failure + continuing disputed redactions + evidence of bad faith + inability to establish that politically sensitive records were handled independently.

That combination could significantly increase the court's willingness to impose stronger compliance measures. It still would not, by itself, establish criminal liability, personal contempt by Blanche, or a legally disqualifying conflict of interest.
