# NM Survivors' Truth Commission

## Seven Key Findings

### Abuse at Zorro Ranch

The New Mexico Survivors' Truth Commission's interim report identifies evidence of abuse involving multiple individuals at Zorro Ranch. The findings describe allegations and testimony that point to serious failures in protecting vulnerable people at the property, raising questions about what occurred there and whether warning signs were adequately investigated.

### Local Recruitment Networks

The report identifies local recruitment networks that allegedly helped facilitate the movement of individuals into the ranch's orbit. This finding suggests that the operation may have depended not only on people connected directly to the property, but also on broader networks within the surrounding community.

### Federal Stonewalling

Another major finding concerns what the commission characterizes as federal stonewalling. According to the interim report, federal authorities failed to provide information or cooperation needed to fully examine the allegations, creating significant obstacles to accountability and leaving important questions unresolved.

### Law Enforcement Failures

The commission also points to failures by law enforcement agencies to adequately investigate or respond to information concerning potential abuse. These shortcomings raise broader concerns about whether authorities recognized warning signs, followed investigative leads, and took appropriate action to protect potential victims.

### Massive Operational Secrecy

The report describes an extensive level of secrecy surrounding activities at Zorro Ranch. Restricted access to information, limited visibility into operations, and the compartmentalization of activities may have made it difficult for outsiders - and potentially authorities - to understand what was taking place on the property.

### Legal Loopholes

The commission identifies weaknesses and loopholes in existing laws that may have enabled individuals involved in the operation to avoid scrutiny or consequences. These gaps demonstrate how legal protections can fail when conduct crosses jurisdictions or falls into areas where existing statutes do not provide clear or effective mechanisms for enforcement.

### Evasion of Sex-Offender Restrictions

Finally, the findings highlight mechanisms that allegedly allowed individuals to evade sex-offender restrictions and related oversight. The commission's concerns suggest that gaps in registration, monitoring, jurisdiction, or enforcement may have enabled continued access to people and places despite legal restrictions intended to prevent further abuse.

# Clarifications: Truth Commission 

I checked the claims in the supplied article against _primary sources_ and _secondary reporting_. The important clarification is that the New Mexico commission's problem is narrower and more concrete than the phrase “federal stonewalling” might suggest: 

- it has received a very large volume of records, but several specific federal subpoenas were not answered, and New Mexico separately says the DOJ has not provided the unredacted investigative material it needs.

## 1. Federal Records Remain Unavailable

The clearest current account comes from the commission's chair, Rep. Andrea Romero, as reported by Reuters.

The commission says _seven of its first 20 subpoenas were refused or ignored_. Two of the most consequential were:

- _Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) flight records_ concerning Epstein-related flights. These are important because flight information could help establish who traveled to Zorro Ranch and when.

- _Records held by the US Attorney's Office for the Southern District of New York (SDNY)_ consisting of investigative material that New Mexico authorities gave federal prosecutors in _2019_. Romero said this material had _never been returned_ to New Mexico.

Reuters reports that the commission had received _more than 100,000 records_ from its first 20 subpoenas, so it is not accurate to characterize the entire federal response as “nothing was provided.” The issue is that particular federal records - and especially records potentially central to the old New Mexico investigation - remain unavailable.

There is a second, related category: _unredacted DOJ/FBI investigative files_ sought by New Mexico Attorney General Raúl Torrez. That is somewhat different from the legislative commission's subpoenas. The state says DOJ has supplied only a small amount of material and that much of it is redacted or already public. DOJ says privacy protections and court orders restrict disclosure, particularly concerning victim identities.

The New Mexico DOJ's own account is particularly useful here. It says that on July 10 it received _31 pages_ from the US Attorney's Office for the District of New Mexico, consisting largely of already-released documents, redacted material, and news clippings; New Mexico says it was still seeking the complete, unredacted investigative record.

_So, in plain English:_ 

- the commission isn't saying “the federal government has given us zero Epstein records.” 

- It is saying that _specific categories of federal evidence - most notably FAA flight records and the 2019 New Mexico investigative material held by SDNY - remain outstanding_, 

- while the Attorney General is separately fighting for broader unredacted federal files.

## 2. 2019 Material Important

New Mexico had already been investigating Zorro Ranch in _2019_. According to former New Mexico Attorney General Hector Balderas, federal prosecutors from SDNY asked New Mexico to stop its parallel sex-trafficking investigation.

The agreement, according to Balderas's July 2026 letter, was essentially:

1. New Mexico would _stop its sex-trafficking investigation_;

2. New Mexico would turn over the evidence it had already gathered;

3. Federal prosecutors would handle the federal case; and

4. At the conclusion of the federal case, SDNY would provide New Mexico with information concerning _state crimes committed in New Mexico_.

Balderas says New Mexico complied by providing investigative material, including victim interviews and police reports, but that the federal side did not subsequently return the promised New Mexico-related material.

Secondary reporting similarly describes the 2019 arrangement as New Mexico agreeing to halt its investigation and provide its evidence to SDNY in exchange for information being shared back that could support state prosecution.

That is why the missing 2019 material matters so much: 

- it isn't simply information New Mexico is asking for years later as an outsider. 

- Some of it was originally New Mexico's own investigative work, which the state says it surrendered because federal prosecutors asked it to stand down.

# 3. “Special Master” and New Mexico 

There is a connection, but _the Special Master proposal does not directly give New Mexico access to its records._

The January 2026 dispute involved a different procedural vehicle: Reps. _Ro Khanna and Thomas Massie_ asked Judge Paul Engelmayer in the federal _United States v. Ghislaine Maxwell_ case to appoint a Special Master or independent monitor to oversee DOJ's compliance with the Epstein Files Transparency Act.

Their argument was essentially:

> DOJ is controlling the production of the files itself, DOJ has not produced everything required, and therefore an independent person should supervise the process.

The proposed Special Master would have been an _independent overseer of DOJ's document-production process_, rather than DOJ being the sole judge of what it should release. Khanna and Massie specifically wanted independent review of the scope of production, redactions and potentially testimony from DOJ officials.

But DOJ's January 16 response argued that the Maxwell criminal case was already closed and that Khanna and Massie were not parties to it. US Attorney Jay Clayton argued that they lacked Article III standing and that Judge Engelmayer lacked authority to turn the closed criminal case into a mechanism for enforcing the Transparency Act.

### Why that matters to New Mexico

The _institutional problem is similar_, even though the legal proceedings are different.

In both situations:

- DOJ possesses potentially relevant Epstein records 

-  an outside party wants access 

-  DOJ says some material cannot be provided 

-  the requesting party argues that DOJ's own control over the records makes independent oversight necessary.

For Khanna and Massie, the proposed solution was a _Special Master_.

### Special Master

For New Mexico, the current solution is _litigation against DOJ_, asking a federal court to compel production of records needed for the state's investigation.

So the Special Master is best understood as _a precedent/contextual parallel_, not as a legal mechanism that New Mexico can simply invoke.

### An important distinction

The Special Master proposal was about _DOJ's nationwide/public release obligations under the Epstein Files Transparency Act_.

New Mexico's present dispute is about _access to evidence for a state investigation into crimes allegedly committed in New Mexico_.

Those are legally different problems.

And DOJ's January argument actually highlights the difficulty: 

- it contended that the Maxwell case could not be used as a vehicle for enforcing the Transparency Act because Congress had not created a corresponding private enforcement mechanism and because the lawmakers weren't parties to the case.

Therefore, even if a Special Master had been appointed in the Maxwell matter, that would not automatically entitle New Mexico to the FAA records or the 2019 SDNY-held investigative file.

What it *would* demonstrate is the larger dispute over whether DOJ should be trusted to decide, without independent judicial oversight, what Epstein-related material other government entities and the public are allowed to see.

## 4. Five Women/girls Statements

There is an important wording issue in the article you supplied.

Reuters reports that the commission's interim findings say _evidence to date indicates Epstein abused at least five women and girls at Zorro Ranch between 1996 and 2012_. The commission also identified _at least 30 additional people_ who may have been victims, witnesses, facilitators, or combinations of those categories.

That should _not_ be paraphrased as “the commission proved that five people were abused.”

The more precise formulation is:

> The commission says the evidence it has collected so far indicates abuse of at least five women and girls, while identifying additional people whose roles or experiences require further investigation.

That distinction is especially important because this is an _interim investigative finding_, not a criminal conviction or judicial determination.

Rachel Benavidez's testimony is one component of that evidence. AP reports that she testified to the commission that Epstein sexually abused her at Zorro Ranch in the late 1990s.

## 5. Local Recruitment Networks

This is another place where I would be cautious with the article's wording.

The broader evidence establishes that Epstein's operation involved _recruitment of young women and girls_, and testimony connected some recruitment to people and organizations outside the ranch itself. But the commission's finding should not automatically be read as having established a specific, fully mapped “New Mexico recruitment network.”

The useful distinction is:

- _Established investigative lead:_ people in the local community may have helped connect victims or potential victims to Epstein's orbit.

- _What investigators still need to establish:_ who specifically recruited whom, whether those people knew the purpose of the recruitment, whether they were paid or otherwise involved, and whether their conduct constituted criminal facilitation.

The commission's identification of roughly _30 additional potential victims/witnesses/facilitators_ is significant because interviewing those people may help answer precisely those questions.

## 6. Law-enforcement Failures 

### 2019 Investigation Was Stopped

New Mexico's investigation was halted after federal prosecutors asked the state to stand down. Balderas now says New Mexico turned over its investigative material with the expectation that state-relevant information would eventually come back.

### Never Searched By Federal Authorities

Contemporary reporting on the newly released files has noted that federal authorities searched other Epstein properties but _did not conduct a comparable search of Zorro Ranch_ before Epstein's death.

### Preserve/seize The Property

Balderas's office later urged federal prosecutors to seize the ranch to preserve potential evidence and offered assistance with search warrants. He says he never learned that federal authorities acted on those requests.

### Federal Subpoenas Are Now Being Ignored Or Refused

That brings the problem into 2026: the commission says seven of its first 20 subpoenas were not complied with, including the FAA and SDNY requests.

So “law enforcement failures” isn't one single event. It describes a _chain of institutional decisions_: the 2019 state investigation being halted, the absence of a comprehensive ranch investigation at that time, the alleged failure to return/share state-relevant material, and current difficulties obtaining federal records.

## 7. Sex-offender-law Issues

There is a concrete statutory issue behind the commission's interest.

New Mexico's own legislative fiscal-impact document for the commission states that _Epstein was a convicted sex offender under Florida law but was not required to register as a sex offender under New Mexico law_. The legislation specifically directed examination of gaps in New Mexico's sex-offender registry laws.

The commission's broader legislative mandate also contemplated examining whether New Mexico's statutes have gaps concerning:

- sex-offender registration;

- monitoring/restrictions;

- jurisdictional problems; and

- statutes of limitation for childhood sexual assault survivors.

I would therefore _not_ characterize the interim findings as already having enacted or definitively recommended a particular new sex-offender restriction unless the final recommendation language is cited directly. The defensible point is that the commission was expressly tasked with examining _gaps in registration and oversight_, prompted in part by the fact that Epstein's Florida conviction did not result in a New Mexico registration requirement.

## Bottom line

The article's “federal stonewalling” finding is best understood as follows:

_New Mexico has obtained a huge quantity of material - more than 100,000 records from its first 20 subpoenas - but some of the most potentially consequential federal records remain unavailable._ The commission specifically identifies the _FAA's Epstein-related flight records_ and _the SDNY-held investigative material New Mexico gave federal prosecutors in 2019_.

The 2019 records are especially significant because New Mexico says _it stopped its own investigation and handed over its evidence at the federal government's request, in an agreement under which relevant state-crime information was supposed to come back to New Mexico._ The state's current lawsuit is, in substantial part, an attempt to force access to that information.

And the _Special Master_ is relevant as a parallel accountability mechanism: Khanna and Massie argued in January that DOJ should not be the sole entity determining whether it has complied with the Epstein disclosure law.

_It does not, however, mean that New Mexico currently has a Special Master overseeing its dispute with DOJ._ New Mexico is pursuing a separate federal lawsuit to obtain the records.
