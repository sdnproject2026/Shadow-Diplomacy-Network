# JPMorgan Chase: Epstein’s Accounts, Transactions and Compliance Failures

Jeffrey Epstein maintained 134 accounts at JPMorgan Chase (JPMC) between 1998 and 2013. These accounts facilitated thousands of wire transfers, massive cash withdrawals, payments to women and girls, and transactions through high‑risk foreign jurisdictions. Despite repeated internal warnings, JPMC failed to report most of this activity to the U.S. Treasury Department until years after Epstein was no longer a client.

## Epstein’s Use of JPMC Accounts

Epstein used his JPMC accounts to:

- withdraw millions of dollars in cash  

- send payments to women and girls around the world  

- move money through correspondent banks in high‑risk jurisdictions  

- pay accomplices, employees and associates  

- transfer tens of millions to Ghislaine Maxwell  

The scale and nature of these transactions should have triggered immediate scrutiny under federal anti‑money‑laundering laws.

## Internal Warnings Ignored

Internal compliance personnel repeatedly raised concerns about Epstein’s accounts. These warnings included:

- unusual cash withdrawals  

- wire transfers lacking clear business purpose  

- payments to individuals in Russia, Belarus and Turkmenistan  

- transfers routed through now‑sanctioned Russian banks  

- patterns consistent with human trafficking indicators  

Despite these red flags, senior JPMC executives protected Epstein as a client.

## Coaching Epstein to Evade Detection

Documents reviewed by investigators show that senior JPMC executives coached Epstein on how to withdraw cash in ways that would avoid detection by compliance personnel. This included:

- using shell companies instead of personal accounts  

- structuring withdrawals to reduce scrutiny  

- routing payments through entities designed to obscure purpose  

These actions helped Epstein conceal information from both the bank’s compliance department and federal regulators.

## JPMC’s Delayed SAR Filings

Although JPMC terminated Epstein as a client in 2013 due to human trafficking concerns, the bank did not file most SARs related to his suspicious activity until 2019 — six years later and only after Epstein’s arrest.

Examples include:

- On September 26, 2019, JPMC retroactively flagged 4,725 wire transfers totaling nearly \$1.1 billion.  

- On August 13, 2019, JPMC flagged 469 additional wire transfers totaling \$201 million, including payments to women in Russia, Belarus and Turkmenistan.  

These filings were not timely under the Bank Secrecy Act, which requires SARs to be filed within 30 days of detecting suspicious activity.

## Continued Relationship After Termination

Even after formally terminating Epstein as a client, senior JPMC executives:

- continued working with Epstein informally  

- used him as a referral source for other ultra‑wealthy clients  

- maintained personal contact with Epstein  

- withheld information from the U.S. government  

This behavior protected Epstein from federal scrutiny and allowed him to continue operating.

## Senior Executives Implicated

Several top JPMC executives were directly involved in handling Epstein’s accounts and failed to report suspicious activity. These individuals include:

- Mary Erdoes  

- Jes Staley  

- Stephen Cutler  

- John Duffy  

- Justin Nelson  

- Paul Barrett  

- Mary Casey  

- David Brigstocke  

- Jeff Matusow  

- Stewart Oldfield  

Many of these executives remain employed in senior positions at JPMC today.

## Summary

JPMC’s failures were not isolated mistakes. They were systemic, repeated and intentional decisions that enabled Epstein’s criminal activity for more than a decade. The bank:

- ignored internal warnings  

- failed to conduct proper due diligence  

- delayed reporting suspicious activity for years  

- protected Epstein because of his wealth and connections  

These failures represent some of the most serious compliance breakdowns uncovered in the investigation.
