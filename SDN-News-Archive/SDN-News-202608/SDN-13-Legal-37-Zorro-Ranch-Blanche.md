## State-Level Criminal vs. Civil Workflow 

Concerning Todd Blanche and Alleged Co-Conspirators

> Important legal qualification: The following is a procedural roadmap, not a conclusion that Todd Blanche or anyone else committed a crime. Allegations in commentary, pleadings, media reports, or investigative materials are not findings of criminal liability.

### Correcting the starting point

As of August 17, 2026, Todd Blanche is the Acting Attorney General of the United States, according to the U.S. Department of Justice. DDepartment of Justice+1

New Mexico's Attorney General, Raúl Torrez, has publicly stated that New Mexico is conducting a criminal investigation concerning alleged criminal activity at Jeffrey Epstein's former Zorro Ranch property. NMDOJ says it reopened that investigation in February 2026 and has repeatedly sought unredacted federal records. NNew Mexico Department of Justice+1

Torrez has not publicly announced that Blanche is a criminal defendant, nor that Blanche has been charged with witness bribery, evidence tampering, or conspiracy. Indeed, Torrez expressly stated that whether the Zorro Ranch investigation ultimately produces an indictment and criminal prosecution remains to be determined. NNew Mexico Department of Justice

That distinction matters enormously.

Also, opening an investigation does not require proof beyond a reasonable doubt. But there is not a single universal rule that "probable cause is required to open every investigation." Different investigative acts have different legal thresholds. For example, a subpoena, search warrant, arrest, indictment, and conviction each involve different procedural and evidentiary requirements.

## Part I

Criminal Workflow

#### 1. Identify the actual alleged offenses

A state prosecutor cannot simply "charge obstruction" or "charge conspiracy" because conduct appears improper.

The investigation first has to identify:

1. The specific New Mexico statute allegedly violated;

2. The elements of that offense;

3. The particular act or omission satisfying each element;

4. The date and location of the conduct;

5. The defendant's required mental state;

6. The evidence establishing each element;

7. Whether New Mexico has jurisdiction; and

8. Whether federal law preempts or immunizes the conduct.

For example, an allegation described publicly as "evidence tampering" would ultimately have to be translated into a specific statutory offense and its statutory elements.

Likewise, "conspiracy" is not simply an accusation that several people acted together. The prosecution would have to establish the elements of the applicable conspiracy statute, including whatever agreement and intent the statute requires.

#### 2. Separate the Epstein/Zorro Ranch investigation from an investigation of Blanche

This is one of the most important distinctions.

### Track A

Underlying Zorro Ranch conduct

Investigators could investigate:

- sexual exploitation;

- trafficking-related conduct;

- recruitment;

- transportation;

- abuse;

- conspiracy;

- accomplices;

- witnesses;

- financial arrangements;

- obstruction occurring during the underlying investigation; and

- other New Mexico offenses.

NMDOJ says its purpose is to identify survivors, witnesses, co-conspirators and other individuals relevant to criminal conduct occurring in New Mexico. NNew Mexico Department of Justice

### Track B

Alleged conduct by federal officials

A separate investigative track would have to determine whether any particular federal official, including Blanche if the evidence actually supported it, committed an independently chargeable offense.

That would require proof connecting the individual personally to the alleged criminal act.

Simply being:

- the head of DOJ;

- responsible for DOJ personnel;

- involved in a records dispute;

- a signatory on a federal filing;

- or responsible for a federal policy

does not by itself establish criminal liability.

### 3. Preserve and obtain evidence

Investigators would ordinarily attempt to preserve:

- federal records;

- state investigative files;

- correspondence;

- emails;

- text messages;

- memoranda;

- subpoenas;

- Touhy requests;

- production logs;

- document-retention records;

- communications concerning redactions;

- communications concerning protective orders;

- witness testimony;

- metadata;

- chain-of-custody information;

- relevant recordings;

- prior sworn testimony; and

- evidence concerning the alleged underlying offenses.

This is particularly important because New Mexico says it has made repeated requests for unredacted federal material and says the July production contained 31 pages consisting largely of already-public or heavily redacted material. NNew Mexico Department of Justice

That allegation can justify further investigation.

It does not, standing alone, prove obstruction or evidence tampering.

### 4. Use compulsory process where legally available

Investigators/prosecutors could potentially seek:

- subpoenas;

- grand-jury subpoenas;

- search warrants;

- preservation orders;

- witness interviews;

- documentary production;

- forensic examination;

- financial records;

- electronic evidence.

But the applicable legal authority and evidentiary threshold must be satisfied for each device.

A subpoena is not equivalent to a search warrant.

A search warrant is not equivalent to an indictment.

An indictment is not equivalent to proof of guilt.

### 5. Determine prosecutorial authority and conflicts

This becomes particularly important if the proposed target is the federal Acting Attorney General.

Ordinarily, New Mexico district attorneys prosecute criminal cases within their districts, while New Mexico law also gives the Attorney General various investigative and prosecutorial powers. NNew Mexico Legislature+1

But if the investigation concerns the New Mexico Attorney General's own federal counterpart or conduct involving a politically sensitive conflict, the prosecutor should address:

- conflicts of interest;

- recusal;

- appointment of a special prosecutor if authorized/appropriate;

- independence of the investigation;

- federal jurisdiction;

- federal-officer immunity;

- removal to federal court;

- Supremacy Clause defenses; and

- separation-of-powers issues.

This is not a routine state criminal prosecution.

### 6. The federal-officer problem

This is potentially the largest legal obstacle to a New Mexico prosecution of Blanche for conduct performed as a federal official.

The Supreme Court's federal-officer immunity doctrine recognizes that a state generally cannot criminally punish a federal officer for conduct that federal law authorized and that was necessary and proper to carrying out federal duties.

The doctrine traces to In re Neagle and has been reaffirmed in later Supreme Court jurisprudence. LLegal Information Institute+1

So the question would not merely be:

> "Did Blanche do something that New Mexico considers unlawful?"

The more difficult question would be:

> "Was the particular conduct outside the lawful scope of his federal authority, rather than conduct authorized by federal law?"

That distinction could determine whether a state prosecution survives at all.

### 7. Decide whether there is probable cause to charge

At this stage investigators would assemble the evidence and prosecutors would determine whether the evidence supports specific offenses.

Possible outcomes include:

### A. No charge

The evidence does not establish a prosecutable offense.

### B. Continue investigation

Evidence suggests wrongdoing but additional proof is necessary.

### C. Charge other participants

Evidence supports charges against individuals other than Blanche.

### D. Charge Blanche

Only if the evidence establishes the elements of a specific offense and the jurisdictional/immunity problems can be overcome.

### E. Refer the matter elsewhere

The evidence may implicate federal crimes, another state, or another prosecuting authority.

### 8. How the actual criminal "charge" would occur

New Mexico permits felony prosecution through mechanisms including an indictment or an information. New Mexico court materials explain that a felony may be prosecuted by indictment or information, and that the prosecutor has discretion concerning the charging mechanism. SSupreme Court+1

### Route 1

Grand-jury indictment

The prosecutor presents evidence to a grand jury.

The grand jury determines whether an indictment should issue.

If an indictment is returned:

1. The charging document is filed.

2. The defendant is brought before the court.

3. The defendant is informed of the charges.

4. Counsel is appointed/retained as applicable.

5. Bail/pretrial-release issues are addressed.

6. The case proceeds toward discovery and motions.

New Mexico law provides for prosecutors to participate in grand-jury proceedings and prepare indictments. NNew Mexico Legislature

### Route 2 — Information/complaint

The prosecutor may proceed through an information or other authorized charging mechanism.

Again, the charging document must identify an actual offense and allege facts sufficient to establish the statutory elements.

### 9. Pretrial litigation

If Blanche were actually charged, expect substantial litigation over:

- federal-officer immunity;

- Supremacy Clause immunity;

- jurisdiction;

- removal;

- venue;

- statute of limitations;

- prosecutorial authority;

- constitutional defenses;

- admissibility of federal records;

- privilege;

- protective orders;

- Brady/Giglio disclosure;

- discovery;

- subpoenas;

- search-and-seizure issues;

- due process;

- selective/prosecutorial vindictiveness arguments;

- conflicts of interest.

A federal official could attempt to move the case into federal court depending upon the precise claims and procedural posture.

## 10. Prosecution / trial

If the case survives dismissal motions, the prosecution would have to prove the charged offenses at trial.

The prosecution would present:

1. Witness testimony;

2. Documents;

3. Electronic communications;

4. Forensic evidence;

5. Expert testimony where appropriate;

6. Authentication evidence;

7. Evidence establishing intent;

8. Evidence establishing the defendant's personal participation; and

9. Evidence establishing every element beyond a reasonable doubt.

The defense would be entitled to:

- cross-examination;

- compulsory process;

- evidentiary objections;

- constitutional challenges;

- presentation of contrary evidence;

- and the presumption of innocence.

## 11. Verdict

The jury could return:

- guilty;

- not guilty; or

- in some circumstances, no unanimous verdict/mistrial.

An indictment itself is not a conviction.

A prosecutor's allegation is not a finding of fact.

A grand jury's indictment is not proof beyond a reasonable doubt.

## 12. Sentencing

Only after a lawful conviction would "sentence" become applicable.

The sentencing process would ordinarily involve:

1. Conviction;

2. Determination of the applicable offense level/statutory penalty;

3. Consideration of sentencing statutes and applicable guidelines/rules;

4. Presentence investigation/report where required;

5. Arguments from prosecution and defense;

6. Victim-impact considerations where legally applicable;

7. Judicial consideration of aggravating/mitigating circumstances;

8. Sentencing hearing;

9. Judgment; and

10. Post-sentence appellate/review rights.

The sentence would depend on the actual offense of conviction, not on the seriousness of the allegations made during the investigation.

## Part II

### Civil Workflow

The civil case is fundamentally different.

New Mexico is presently pursuing the federal-records dispute through legal action and related demands rather than announcing a criminal prosecution of Blanche personally. NMDOJ has repeatedly described the records dispute as obstructing its criminal investigation. NNew Mexico Department of Justice+1

### 1. Establish a civil cause of action

A civil plaintiff must identify an actual legal basis for relief.

Potential theories can include, depending upon the precise facts and jurisdiction:

- declaratory relief;

- injunctive relief;

- mandamus-type relief where legally available;

- statutory enforcement;

- administrative-law claims;

- federal-question claims;

- constitutional claims;

- records-access claims.

The state cannot simply file a lawsuit asking a court to declare that someone "covered up Epstein."

The complaint must identify a legally cognizable claim.

### 2. Identify the proper defendant

This is critical.

The civil defendant might be:

- the United States;

- DOJ;

- a federal official in an official capacity;

- another federal agency;

- or another legally proper party.

Suing an official personally is different from suing that official in an official capacity.

The distinction affects:

- sovereign immunity;

- available remedies;

- jurisdiction;

- damages;

- injunctive relief;

- and whether the United States must be treated as the real party in interest.

### 3. File the complaint

The complaint would state:

1. Jurisdiction;

2. Parties;

3. Factual allegations;

4. Legal claims;

5. Injury or legal interest;

6. Requested relief.

For example, the requested relief might seek an order requiring production of particular records rather than criminal punishment.

### 4. Seek preliminary relief

If delay is causing ongoing harm, the plaintiff could seek emergency or preliminary relief where the applicable legal standards are met.

The court would consider the applicable test, potentially including:

- likelihood of success;

- irreparable harm;

- balance of equities;

- and public interest.

The court—not the prosecutor—decides whether an injunction issues.

### 5. Federal jurisdiction and immunity

Because the opposing party may be the United States or federal officials acting officially, the court would have to resolve:

- sovereign immunity;

- statutory waiver of immunity;

- federal-question jurisdiction;

- standing;

- ripeness;

- justiciability;

- federal preemption;

- separation of powers;

- privileges;

- protective orders;

- and applicable federal records law.

This is why the civil route can be substantially different from simply filing a state criminal complaint.

### 6. Discovery / record production

If discovery is permitted, the parties may litigate over:

- document requests;

- interrogatories;

- depositions;

- subpoenas;

- privilege;

- redactions;

- protective orders;

- declarations;

- affidavits;

- and motions to compel.

A court can order production when legally justified.

But it can also uphold withholding or redaction when federal law, privilege, protective orders, privacy interests, or other applicable doctrines require it.

### 7. Merits decision

The civil court could ultimately:

- order production;

- deny production;

- declare the parties' legal rights;

- dismiss claims;

- grant summary judgment;

- deny summary judgment;

- issue an injunction;

- or provide another legally authorized remedy.

A civil judgment does not automatically establish criminal guilt.

### 8. Civil enforcement versus criminal punishment

This distinction is especially important for the proposed Blanche theory.

### Civil case

The state might seek:

> "Order DOJ to produce the records."

### Criminal case

The state would have to prove:

> "This individual personally committed a specifically defined criminal offense, with the required mens rea and jurisdiction."

### Sentence

Only after conviction:

> "What punishment does the criminal law authorize for the offense proved?"

Those are three separate legal questions.

## Part III

### What Would Have To Happen To Get From "allegation" To "sentence"

## Criminal Workflow

- Public allegations

  - Claims or allegations enter the public record.

  - Allegations are not themselves evidence sufficient for conviction.

- State investigation

  - Investigators assess whether the allegations are supported by admissible or otherwise lawfully obtainable evidence.

  - If the evidence does not substantiate a prosecutable offense:

    - Close investigation / no charge.

  - If the evidence potentially substantiates an offense:

    - Continue to the next stage.

- Identify specific statutes

  - Determine which specific criminal statutes could apply.

  - Identify the required elements of each offense.

- Establish personal conduct

  - Determine what the particular individual allegedly did or directed.

  - Establish the individual's personal connection to the alleged criminal conduct.

- Establish intent and statutory elements

  - Determine whether the required mental state exists.

  - Establish evidence supporting every required element of the offense.

- Resolve jurisdiction

  - Determine whether the state has authority to prosecute the alleged conduct.

  - Address venue, territorial jurisdiction, and other jurisdictional issues.

- Resolve federal-officer immunity / preemption

  - Determine whether the alleged conduct was performed pursuant to federal authority.

  - Address federal-officer immunity, Supremacy Clause issues, federal preemption, and related defenses.

- Prosecutorial decision

  - No charge

    - Investigation may be closed or continued.

  - Charge

    - Proceed to formal criminal charging.

- Indictment / information

  - Prosecutor proceeds through the legally available charging mechanism.

  - A grand jury may return an indictment where that procedure is used.

  - Alternatively, an information may be filed where authorized.

- Arraignment

  - Defendant appears before the court.

  - Charges are formally presented.

  - Counsel and pretrial-release issues are addressed.

- Pretrial litigation

  - Discovery.

  - Motions to dismiss.

  - Immunity and jurisdictional challenges.

  - Evidentiary disputes.

  - Constitutional challenges.

  - Other pretrial motions.

- Trial

  - Prosecution presents its evidence.

  - Defense presents its case as appropriate.

  - Prosecution must prove the charged offenses beyond a reasonable doubt.

- Verdict

  - Acquittal

    - Defendant is found not guilty.

  - Conviction

    - Defendant is found guilty of one or more charged offenses.

    - Proceed to sentencing.

- Sentencing

  - Court determines the legally authorized punishment for the offense(s) of conviction.

  - Sentencing considerations may include statutory penalties and applicable sentencing rules.

- Appeal / review

  - Defendant may pursue available appellate or post-conviction remedies.

  - The prosecution may have limited appellate rights depending on the issue and applicable law.
