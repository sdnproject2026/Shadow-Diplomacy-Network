## Statutory Interpretation

The US Court of Appeals for the DC Circuit is reviewing the government’s interlocutory appeal on jurisdictional limits and statutory interpretation under the Epstein Files Transparency Act, evaluating whether unredacted FBI 302 notes fall under preliminary injunction rules or discovery mandates.

Legal analysts expect the court to determine whether the explicit disclosure requirements of the statute supersede standard executive deliberative process privileges.

The DC Circuit is likely to apply a functional "practical effect" test under 28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(1), rather than simply accepting Judge Sullivan’s label for the order.

The key DC Circuit precedent is especially helpful here:

- In Emmet Sullivan's current Phang v. Blanche litigation, the July 25, 2026 order directed the government to submit specified materials for in camera review.

- The docket reflects that order as separate from Sullivan's June 25 preliminary injunction.

- The DC - Circuit has said an injunction under § 1292(a)(1) encompasses an order "directed to a party, enforceable by contempt, and designed to accord or protect some or all of the substantive relief sought by a complaint." IAM National Pension Fund Benefit Plan A v. Cooper Industries, 789 F.2d 21, 24 (DC - Cir. 1986).

- That formulation was applied in Hinton v. DOJ, where the court rejected immediate appeal of an order requiring the FBI to prepare a Vaughn index.

- Even though compliance imposed enormous costs and the order compelled government action, it was a discovery/information-gathering device, not an injunction providing or protecting the substantive relief sought.

- The court warned that treating every compelled action as an injunction would make "every discovery order" immediately appealable.

- Likewise, in CREW v. DHS, the DC - Circuit held that an order requiring the government to process a records request was not an injunction because it merely required the agency to search for records and determine what could lawfully be withheld; it did not itself require disclosure.

- Conversely, in Investment Company Institute v. FDIC, the court found an order appealable because its central provision required action unrelated to the conduct or management of the litigation and was essentially a substantive mandate to compel agency action.

### Applied to Sullivan's in-camera order

So the DC Circuit's inquiry should be roughly:

1. What does the order actually do, rather than what is it called?

2. Does it alter or protect the parties' substantive legal rights, or merely regulate the litigation's information-gathering process?

3. Is the government being ordered to do something that constitutes substantive relief under the complaint/injunction?

4. Or is the order simply a mechanism by which Sullivan obtains information so he can later decide whether the government must disclose it?

### That last distinction is probably decisive

If the July 25 order merely says, in substance, "give the court these documents privately so I can determine whether your redactions/withholdings comply with the Act, " it looks strongly like a discovery or case-management order under Hinton and CREW.

The fact that Sullivan can enforce compliance through contempt is not enough; Hinton expressly recognized that feature but still treated the order as non-injunctive.

If, however, the order is properly understood as implementing or enforcing the June 25 preliminary injunction-for example, if the in-camera submission is merely an intermediate step in an already-entered command requiring substantive disclosure, then the government has a stronger argument that the order is part of an appealable interlocutory injunction.

Bottom line: 

- the DC Circuit should focus on the order's substantive legal effect, using the IAM National Pension Fund/Hinton formulation.

- The central question is whether the in-camera review itself accords or protects substantive relief, or merely facilitates Sullivan's adjudication of whether such relief is warranted.

- On the facts currently reflected in the docket, there is a substantial argument that the July 25 order is the latter-and therefore an unappealable discovery order, notwithstanding its connection to the June 25 injunction.

## In-camera

What is Sullivan's strongest argument that the order is an ordinary, unappealable discovery or case-management order?

Sullivan's strongest argument is that the in-camera review order is procedural rather than substantive: it does not require the government to disclose aNYThing to the plaintiffs, alter the parties' legal rights, or grant the substantive relief sought.

It merely requires the government to provide materials to the court so that Sullivan can decide what, if aNYThing, must happen next.

The argument can be framed in five parts:

### The order does not itself grant relief. 

An interlocutory injunction under 28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(1) ordinarily must accord or protect some portion of the substantive relief sought.

Sullivan can argue that an order requiring confidential submission to the judge does none of that.

The plaintiffs receive no documents, the government is not ordered to disclose them publicly, and no ultimate obligation concerning the documents has yet been imposed.

### In-camera review is an adjudicative mechanism. 

The order is analogous to a discovery or evidentiary procedure used to enable the court to determine whether the government's asserted withholding, redaction, or privilege is legally justified.

Its purpose is to inform the court's eventual ruling, not to provide the plaintiffs with substantive relief.

### The order does not determine the merits. 

Sullivan can emphasize that the government retains its substantive position concerning disclosure until the court rules on it.

The in-camera submission therefore does not conclusively determine whether the government must produce the materials.

It simply puts the disputed materials before the decision-maker.

### Contempt power does not convert the order into an injunction. 

The government may argue that because Sullivan's order is mandatory and enforceable, it has the characteristics of an injunction.

Sullivan's answer would be that Hinton v.

DOJ rejects that reasoning: discovery orders can be mandatory and enforceable without becoming immediately appealable injunctions.

Otherwise, virtually every coercive discovery order against a government defendant could generate an interlocutory appeal.

### The order is distinguishable from an order compelling substantive government action. 

Sullivan's best distinction from cases such as Investment Company Institute v.

FDIC is that the government has not been ordered to take the ultimate action at issue.

It has been ordered only to show the court the materials necessary for the court to decide whether that ultimate action is required.

## The strongest formulation

The most powerful version of Sullivan's position would therefore be:

> The order is not an injunction because it neither gives the plaintiffs aNYThing nor takes away any substantive legal right.

It simply requires the government to submit disputed materials to the court for confidential judicial inspection.

Until Sullivan decides what those materials establish and enters an order concerning disclosure, the government's substantive obligations remain unresolved.

That formulation tracks the central concern in Hinton: if every order compelling a party to perform an intermediate procedural act were treated as an injunction, the line between discovery and § 1292(a)(1) injunctions would effectively disappear.

### The important qualification

There is, however, a potential weakness in Sullivan's argument: context matters.

If the July 25 order is not an independent discovery mechanism but instead is an implementation of the June 25 preliminary injunction, the government can argue that the two orders must be treated together.

In that circumstance, the government could characterize the in-camera submission as a necessary enforcement step toward the substantive relief already ordered.

Sullivan's response would be that implementation does not equal substantive relief.

Even if the in-camera review relates to the preliminary injunction, the particular order under review should remain nonappealable unless it independently has the character of an injunction or actually modifies/enforces substantive rights.

## Bottom line

Sullivan's strongest position is therefore not that the order lacks coercive force.

It is that coercion is beside the point.

The decisive distinction is between:

> Produce these materials to the court so I can decide what relief, if any, is appropriate

and

> Take this substantive action that gives the plaintiffs the relief they seek.

The first is a discovery/case-management order; the second is much more likely to be an appealable interlocutory injunction under § 1292(a)(1).

## Answer

Based on the statutory framework described above, DOJ would not automatically be compelled to release literally every unreleased document or remove every redaction. The stronger legal proposition is:

> DOJ must produce every responsive, covered record in its possession unless the government establishes a specific withholding or redaction permitted by § 2(c) of the Epstein Files Transparency Act, and even then only the legally protected portion may be withheld or redacted if the remainder is segregable.

That distinction is critical.

### The basic categories

- Responsive + unclassified + no § 2(c) exception

  - Generally subject to production.

- Victim-identifying information

  - Protected information may be redacted.

  - Segregable non-protected material should remain subject to disclosure.

- Child sexual abuse material

  - Permitted withholding/redaction.

- Active federal investigation or ongoing prosecution

  - Withholding is potentially permitted if disclosure would jeopardize the investigation/prosecution.

  - The withholding must be narrowly tailored and temporary.

- Images depicting death, physical abuse, or injury

  - Permitted withholding/redaction.

- Properly classified national-defense or foreign-policy information

  - Declassification is required to the maximum extent possible.

  - If information genuinely cannot be declassified, the Act provides for an unclassified summary.

- Embarrassing information

  - Not a statutory basis for withholding.

- Reputationally damaging information

  - Not a statutory basis for withholding.

- Political sensitivity

  - Not a statutory basis for withholding.

- Internal DOJ document

  - Not automatically exempt.

- FBI document

  - Not automatically exempt.

- Law-enforcement sensitivity alone

  - Not one of the five specified § 2(c) categories.

- Deliberative-process privilege alone

  - Potentially insufficient if the Act displaces that privilege.

  - The precise interaction between the Act and common-law privileges remains a potentially decisive legal question.

- Attorney-client privilege alone

  - Same basic issue: whether the privilege independently survives the Act.

- Work-product privilege alone

  - Same basic issue.

## What DOJ would have to establish

For each disputed document or redaction, the court could require DOJ to establish:

- Coverage

  - The material is actually within the Act's definition of covered records.

- Statutory basis for withholding

  - The particular withheld information falls within an authorized § 2(c) category.

- Causation where required

  - For example, DOJ must show that disclosure would actually jeopardize an active investigation or ongoing prosecution.

- Narrow tailoring

  - The withholding must be no broader than the statutory exception permits.

- Temporariness

  - Particularly important for active-investigation material.

- Segregability

  - Non-protected portions must be released where they can be separated from protected information.

- Written justification

  - DOJ must provide the statutorily required justification for redactions.

## The "segregable portions" issue

This could be one of the most important mechanisms for obtaining additional unredacted material.

Suppose an FBI document contains:

- non-protected investigative information; 

- a victim's identifying information; and

- genuinely classified information.

The statutory framework would point toward:

- release of the non-protected investigative information; 

- redaction of the qualifying victim-identifying information; 

- treatment of genuinely classified material under the Act's classification provisions.

The government could not necessarily withhold the entire document simply because one portion qualifies for protection.

That is why the question is not merely:

> "Is this document exempt?"

It is more precisely:

> "Which particular information in this document is legally protected, and what non-protected material can be segregated and released?"

## Why the FBI 302 notes matter

The dispute over handwritten FBI interview notes illustrates why Sullivan ordered in-camera review.

The court can compare:

- the raw interview notes; 

- the formal FBI 302; 

- the redacted version of the 302; 

- DOJ's stated justification for each redaction.

The court can then determine:

- what the underlying record actually says; 

- whether the 302 accurately reflects it; 

- whether the redacted information falls within a statutory exception; 

- whether the redaction is broader than necessary; and

- whether additional portions must be disclosed.

That makes the in-camera review potentially significant even if the review order itself is ultimately classified as a discovery/case-management order.

## What could produce an order for essentially all remaining records?

A particularly broad production order would likely require findings along these lines:

### Finding 1: The records are covered

The court determines that the disputed records fall within the categories of records covered by the Act.

### Finding 2: The Act controls

The court determines that the Act's disclosure regime governs the records rather than allowing ordinary FOIA principles or executive privileges to create additional withholding categories.

### Finding 3: No statutory exception applies

DOJ cannot establish that the particular material falls within one of the Act's permitted withholding categories.

### Finding 4: Protected material is segregable

Even where an exception applies, non-protected material can be separated and released.

### Finding 5: DOJ's justification is inadequate

DOJ cannot adequately establish the legal basis for a particular withholding or redaction.

### Finding 6: Temporary withholding has expired

For material withheld because of an active investigation or prosecution, the statutory justification no longer exists or DOJ cannot establish that disclosure would presently jeopardize the proceeding.

If those findings were applied across the entire disputed universe, the practical result could be production of essentially all remaining responsive records, with only specifically protected portions withheld.

## What could produce an "unredact everything" order?

The analysis is somewhat different for redactions.

Sullivan would not necessarily need to determine that the entire document is unrestricted.

He could instead determine:

> The particular redacted information does not fall within a legally valid withholding category.

That could support an order requiring DOJ to reproduce the document without that redaction.

For example, if DOJ redacts a person's name solely because disclosure could cause embarrassment or reputational damage, that justification would face a serious statutory problem because those considerations are not themselves permitted withholding grounds.

But a person's identity could still be protected if disclosure would reveal qualifying victim-identifying information or otherwise fall within a valid statutory exception.

Thus the operative question is:

> Does this particular redacted information independently fall within a permitted withholding category?

If not, the statutory basis for the redaction disappears.

## The privilege question

This is potentially the most consequential issue for the remaining documents.

If DOJ says:

> "We are withholding this because it is deliberative."

the court has to ask:

> Does deliberative-process privilege independently authorize withholding under this statute?

If the answer is no, then:

- covered record; 

- unclassified; 

- no § 2(c) exception; 

- no surviving independent privilege

could mean:

> production required.

The same conceptual analysis applies to attorney-client and work-product claims.

But it is important not to state that Sullivan has necessarily resolved this question for every document. The precise scope of privilege abrogation or displacement can depend on the statutory text, the particular privilege asserted, and applicable precedent.

## The strongest possible enforcement order

If Sullivan ultimately adopts the broadest interpretation of the Act, an enforcement order could require DOJ to:

- identify every responsive record in DOJ's possession; 

- produce every record not covered by a valid statutory exception; 

- provide sufficiently particularized explanations for disputed withholdings; 

- identify the statutory basis for each redaction; 

- remove unsupported redactions; 

- release all segregable non-protected portions; 

- periodically reassess material withheld under the temporary active-investigation exception; 

- declassify classified material to the maximum extent possible; 

- provide required unclassified summaries where appropriate; 

- submit disputed material for in-camera review; 

- publish or provide the required redaction justifications; and

- certify compliance to the court.

At that point, the order would look substantially more like a substantive statutory-compliance injunction than ordinary discovery.

That distinction connects directly to the § 1292(a)(1) issue.

In Hinton, the information-gathering mechanism was treated as procedural. An order actually requiring the requested substantive disclosure presents a materially different situation.

## The critical limitation

The legally strongest ultimate order would probably not say:

> "DOJ shall release everything."

It would instead say, in substance:

> DOJ shall release every covered record and every segregable portion of a covered record unless DOJ establishes that the particular material falls within one of the specific withholding provisions enacted by Congress.

That formulation respects the statutory exceptions while preventing DOJ from converting those exceptions into blanket exemptions.

## Bottom line

The decisive framework is:

For an unreleased document:

> Covered record + unclassified + no valid statutory exception + no surviving independent privilege = production.

For a redaction:

> Redacted information + no valid statutory exception + no surviving independent privilege = unredaction.

For a partially protected document:

> Protected material + segregable non-protected material = redact the protected material, release the remainder.

For an active-investigation claim:

> Actual active investigation/prosecution + disclosure would jeopardize it + narrow tailoring + temporary withholding = potential withholding.

For classification:

> Classified information + maximum possible declassification + unclassified summary where necessary = limited withholding rather than categorical secrecy.

The central battleground is therefore not simply whether DOJ possesses more documents. It is whether DOJ can establish a legally valid basis for each remaining withholding or redaction under the Act.

If Sullivan rejects DOJ's asserted privileges as independent grounds for withholding and requires document-specific compliance with the Act's exceptions and segregability requirement, the scope of compelled production could become very broad.
