# Ancient Levantine

## Canaanite

Canaanite religion was practiced by West Semitic peoples along the Levantine coast and inland (roughly modern Israel, Palestine, Lebanon, and western Syria).

It featured a pantheon headed by El and including Baal, Asherah, Anat, Astarte, and others, with myths preserved in the Ugaritic tablets from Ras Shamra that describe divine kingship, seasonal cycles, and cosmic battles.

## Ugaritic (Ugarit)

The city-state of Ugarit preserved one of the richest corpora of Bronze Age Levantine religious texts, revealing detailed myths and rituals.

Its cults centered on El as high god and Baal as storm and fertility deity, with temples to Baal and Dagan and rituals tied closely to agriculture and royal ideology.

## Phoenician

Phoenician religion grew from the broader Canaanite tradition and spread across the Mediterranean through cities such as Tyre, Sidon, and Byblos and colonies like Carthage.

Key deities included Baal (often Baal Shamem or Baal Hammon), Astarte, Melqart, and Eshmun, and cult practices emphasized temple offerings, votive dedications, and, in some regions, controversial rites later condemned by Greek and Roman authors.

## Israelite/Yahwistic

Early Israelite religion emerged in the same Levantine environment and in its earliest forms shows continuity with broader West Semitic polytheism.

Over time it developed toward exclusive worship of Yahweh, strongly polemicizing against Baal and other Canaanite gods, and reinterpreting older traditions within a developing monotheistic framework.

## Aramean

Aramean polities in inland Syria and northern Mesopotamia shared many West Semitic deities, venerating Hadad (a storm god closely related to Baal), El, and local patron gods.

Their religion illustrates how Baal/Hadad-type storm deities functioned as royal protectors and guarantors of fertility across a wide swath of the Levant.

## Moabite, Ammonite, and Edomite

Neighboring kingdoms east and south of Israel - Moab, Ammon, and Edom - had their own state gods like Chemosh (Moab), Milkom (Ammon), and Qos (Edom).

While each had distinct patron deities, their religious concepts and practices show strong overlap with other Levantine traditions, including sacrificial rites and veneration of high places.

## Baal (Baʿal)

Baal, whose name or title means “lord” or “owner” in Northwest Semitic languages, was primarily a Canaanite-Phoenician storm and fertility god associated with rain, thunder, lightning, agricultural abundance, and royal power.

In Ugaritic myth he battles the sea god Yam and the death god Mot, his victories and defeats mirroring seasonal cycles of rain, drought, fertility, and famine in the Levant, and his cult spread widely across the region and into Mediterranean Phoenician colonies.
