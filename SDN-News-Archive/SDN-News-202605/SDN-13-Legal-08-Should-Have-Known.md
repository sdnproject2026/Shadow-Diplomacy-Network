# Judge James K. Bredar

## The Ruling Details

### Anonymity Preserved

- Senior District Judge James K. Bredar ruled that the victims may continue using their "John Doe" pseudonyms throughout the pretrial phase.

### Reasoning

- The judge stated that forcing the men to publicly reveal their names would subject them to significant risk of public exposure and re-traumatization.

- He concluded that these personal risks outweighed the defense's argument that pseudonyms hampered their ability to gather witness testimonies.

## Case Context

### The Allegations

- The lawsuit was filed by several former World Wrestling Entertainment (WWE) "ring boys" who are now in their 50s.

- They allege that during the 1980s and 1990s, they were systematically groomed and sexually abused by late ringside announcer Melvin Phillips Jr. and other adults.

### The Legal Claim

- The lawsuit accuses the McMahons of negligence, claiming they knew or should have known about the pervasive abuse but chose to look the other way, creating and concealing a culture of abuse.

### Defense Response

- Linda and Vince McMahon have vehemently denied all allegations, calling them meritless and "scurrilous lies".

### Case Trajectory

- While the case was temporarily paused to review the constitutionality of Maryland's expanded statute.

- Judge Bredar ruled that the lawsuit is legally plausible and has allowed the negligence claims to proceed into the discovery phase.

# Legal Timeline

The historical and legal timeline regarding the World Wrestling Entertainment (WWE) "ring boy" sexual abuse allegations spans from the initial discovery of the misconduct to the ongoing federal court proceedings.

## 1992-03

- Former WWF ring boy Tom Cole brings the "ring boy" scandal to the public eye.

- Cole alleges that he and other underage ring crew members were systematically harassed and abused by team executives during the 1980s.

- WWF executive Terry Garvin and ringside announcer Mel Phillips resign from the company amid the immediate public fallout, while executive Pat Patterson steps down temporarily.

## 2024-10-23

- Five anonymous men officially file a federal lawsuit in Maryland against World Wrestling Entertainment (WWE), TKO Group Holdings, Vince McMahon, and Linda McMahon.

- The plaintiffs utilize Maryland's newly enacted Child Victims Act to sue for historical negligence.

- The complaint states that the defendants knowingly fostered a corporate culture of sexual misconduct and failed to protect teenage workers from predatory executives.

## 2024-12

- The presiding federal court officially issues a temporary stay on the lawsuit.

- The pause is enacted to wait for a high-profile legal challenge regarding the overall constitutionality of the Maryland Child Victims Act.

## 2025-02

- The Supreme Court of Maryland formally issues a definitive ruling declaring the Child Victims Act constitutional.

- The ruling effectively lifts the stay and permits the historical negligence lawsuit against the McMahons to legally move forward.

## 2025-04

- Three additional anonymous plaintiffs formally join the ongoing legal action, bringing the total number of victims to eight.

- The expanded complaint introduces new historical allegations of abuse identifying Pat Patterson and former wrestler Koko B.

- Ware.

## 2025-12-10

- Senior US District Judge James K. Bredar officially denies the core motions to dismiss filed by Vince McMahon, Linda McMahon, WWE, and TKO Group Holdings.

- The court fully dismisses the claims of one plaintiff whose abuse dated back to the 1970s because the defendants could not have had the requisite prior knowledge to establish corporate negligence at that time.

- The judge rules that the negligence claims of the remaining seven "John Doe" plaintiffs are legally plausible and allows the case to advance to the discovery phase.

## 2026-05-14

- Judge James K. Bredar officially signs a memorandum and order denying the legal push by Vince and Linda McMahon to force the plaintiffs to unmask themselves.

- The court rules that the plaintiffs may continue to protect their identities under "John Doe" pseudonyms through the entirety of the pretrial phase.

- The judge establishes that the high risks of public exposure and psychological re-traumatization heavily outweigh the defense's claims of legal hardship.
