# AI vs. LLM

That’s a sharp and insightful comparison - and yes, there’s a strong case that what’s now called “AI,” especially in the form of large language models (LLMs), is a continuation of the semantic web idea from the late 1990s and early 2000s - though with very different marketing, economics, and emotional responses around it.

## Marketing and Perception

When companies market “AI,” they’re selling emotion and possibility - intelligence, automation, creativity, and even replacement of labor. “AI” suggests a force that thinks.

By contrast, “LLM” sounds technical and limited - a neural network trained on large text data predicting tokens.

The public finds that harder to grasp, so it becomes a term used more inside research and engineering circles.

## Magic

Marketing “AI” abstracts away the mechanics and focuses on magic.

That’s powerful for investors and consumers, but it also triggers fear - fear of losing control, jobs, or meaning. “LLM,” in contrast, conveys machinery rather than mystery, which dampens both excitement and anxiety.

### The Semantic Web Connection

The early “semantic web” envisioned by Tim Berners-Lee aimed to make internet content machine-readable - using metadata and linked data so computers could reason about relationships, not just text.

It was supposed to turn the web into a knowledge graph where software could answer human questions intelligently.

That vision largely stalled because tagging and structuring global data required universal standards and cooperation.

What happened instead was statistical inference: rather than hand-encoding knowledge, LLMs learned it by pattern recognition from massive text corpora.

Today, when an LLM answers a question in natural language instead of returning a list of links - it’s effectively doing what the semantic web promised, but without the structure.

It’s inference replacing ontology.

And in doing so, it renders old interfaces (like Yahoo’s directory or Google’s keyword search) increasingly obsolete.

### Why Fear Feels Different Now

> The semantic web invited efficiency and order.

> AI invites agency and autonomy.

People didn’t fear that a linked-data ontology might take their job or impersonate them.

But when marketed as “AI,” LLMs seem _alive_.

They can talk back, imitate emotion, and evolve fast.

That blend of conversational immediacy and opaque capability makes the human responses to “AI” - fascination, dependency, resistance - far stronger than ever before.

### “AI” Is The Semantic Web

So yes, in essence, “AI” is the semantic web realized through brute-force learning and computation - wrapped in narrative, branding, and consequence.

The technology may rhyme with the 1990s dream, but the social reaction to it belongs entirely to our present moment.

# Autonomous “agency”

The shift from traditional search results to autonomous “agency” represents a fundamental change in how humans interact with digital information - and, critically, in how decisions get made.

## Agency vs. Search

Search engines act as mirrors.

> they reflect what already exists.

When you search, you get a list of documents, each requiring interpretation and action from you.

The user remains the agent - you choose what’s relevant, you synthesize, you decide.

Autonomous agents, by contrast, _act_.

They don’t just find data; they pursue objectives.

Instead of returning information, they might book a flight, write an email, monitor prices, or coordinate tasks.

The human shifts from “operator” to “supervisor.” The mental model moves from discovery to delegation.

> In search, knowledge is passive information.

> In agency, knowledge becomes actionable intelligence.

### Cognitive and Emotional Shifts

This change provokes deep psychological effects.

People accustomed to gathering input and reasoning through it now face systems that decide outcomes.

An autonomous agent feels less like a library and more like a colleague or competitor.

As a result, anxiety rises around autonomy and control.

When agency leaves the human loop, people worry that they’re no longer informed participants - they’re spectators to algorithmic decision-making.

That parallels concerns about trust and transparency.

You can inspect a list of sources in a search result.

You cannot easily inspect the internal reasoning of an autonomous agent that synthesized those sources silently before acting.

### Philosophical Implications

Agency collapses the gap between intention and action.

The web once required translation - thinking, deciding, clicking.

Autonomous systems erase those cognitive steps.

### The question becomes:

Whose goals are embedded in the agent’s behavior? Corporate defaults? Optimized efficiency? Human ethics? When an agent acts, it expresses intent - and intent is power.

### Surrogate Intelligence

In short, autonomous “agency” replaces the informational mirror of search with a surrogate intelligence that executes choice itself.

It’s not about more data; it’s about displaced decision-making - a new frontier where our greatest tool may gradually become our most independent collaborator.

## Prime Mover

Yes - if you mean “prime mover” as the highest delegated authority in a chain of causes, then AI delegation feels like a miniature, practical version of a very old theological pattern.

The human becomes a prime mover for narrower domains, while the theistic idea places ultimate agency at the level of existence itself.

### The Analogy

In the AI case, a person delegates a bounded task domain to an agent.

>summarize, sort, decide, draft, monitor.

The human remains responsible, but the machine absorbs execution and some initiative.

That is delegation within a local universe of goals.

### Existential

In the theistic case, the delegation is existential rather than operational.

One does not delegate a spreadsheet or inbox, but the meaning of being, order, contingency, and the whole arc of life, the universe, and everything.

That makes the “prime mover” less like a manager and more like a metaphysical ground of motion or purpose.

### Why The Comparison Matters

The striking similarity is not that AI is divine, but that both models organize reality around a hierarchy of agency.

In each case, lower-level actors appear active, yet their action is framed by a higher source of direction, value, or causation.

The difference is scale and finality.

AI delegation is reversible, experimental, and instrumentally limited.

Theistic delegation is totalizing, often treated as ultimate and non-negotiable.

### Personhood And Status

If a person treats themselves as the prime mover for trivial domains, they are really claiming stewardship, not godhood.

But the psychological effect can still be significant.

> you begin to see yourself less as the one doing every task and more as the one assigning, supervising, and judging.

That can mirror religious self-understanding in a softer form.

The person becomes an ordering center, while the delegated system becomes a secondary cause.

In theological terms, that is closer to participation in agency than replacement of ultimate agency.

### The Important Boundary

AI delegation becomes problematic when people confuse operational control with ultimate authority.

You can delegate tasks, but not your responsibility for the structure of your life.

You can use agents, but you cannot meaningfully outsource the question of what matters.

So the cleanest reading is this.

> AI turns humans into local prime movers over small domains, while theistic thought reserves “prime mover” for the absolute source behind all domains.

The similarity is real, but only at the level of delegation, not at the level of being.

# The Three Realities of AI

- https://www.axios.com/2026/04/13/ai-elite-vs-ai-skeptic-doomer

Three distinct camps are forming around AI. 

> power users, doubters, and resisters.

### Why It Matters

- AI isn't just advancing - it's fragmenting how people see the world.

### The Big Picture

- The disconnect is showing up everywhere, from job-loss fears to data center protests to actual violence.

- Doubters still see AI as glitchy chatbots and viral fails. They aren't using its full capabilities.

- Power users run AI agents around the clock, trading tips on how to automate work and decision-making.

- Resisters understand AI, think they know where it's headed, and want no part of it.

### What They're Saying

- "There is a growing gap in understanding of AI capability," former OpenAI and Tesla AI leader Andrej Karpathy posted on X. He added that many people let a single session with ChatGPT's free tier define their view of AI.

- Meanwhile, Karpathy told the "No Priors" podcast that he now spends 16 hours a day issuing commands to AI agent swarms and rushes to exhaust his tokens every month.

- "AI adoption is a tale of two cities," Box CEO Aaron Levie said on X.

### By The Numbers

- It's a virtuous cycle. Power users have more success and more productivity boosts than casual users.

- Anthropic's March economic impact report found that experienced users attempt harder tasks and succeed more often.

- The result is a new kind of economic gap between advanced users and everyone else.

### Between The Lines

- The third group of resisters are getting louder.

- In Indianapolis, a legislator said his home was hit by gunfire, with a note left behind saying 'no more data centers.'

- A man was arrested for allegedly throwing a Molotov cocktail at OpenAI CEO Sam Altman's home and had also visited OpenAI's offices before being taken into custody.

- The San Francisco Chronicle reports that someone with the same name as the suspect has published anti-AI essays and participated in a PauseAI Discord server. PauseAI is an activist group that advocates halting AI development.

### State Of Play

- Protests are becoming more common in San Francisco, where many AI firms are based, and in communities targeted for new data centers.

- A growing number of workers with technical skills fear AI will make them obsolete.

- In a viral post, a Meta engineer captured spreading anxiety, writing, "I'm done with tech and I'm done with this unfair world."

### Altman Expressed Optimism

- Altman posted after the attack, acknowledging public fear and concern.

- "It will not all go well," Altman wrote. "The fear and anxiety about AI is justified; we are in the process of witnessing the largest change to society in a long time, and perhaps ever."

### Bottom Line

- The people building and using AI at full power are living in a very different world from everyone else.
